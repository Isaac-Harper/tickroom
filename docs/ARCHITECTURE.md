# Architecture

Why every load-bearing decision in tickroom is what it is, and what breaks when it gets simplified.

Everything here was learned in production, most of it the expensive way. If you are about to change something in this library and it looks gratuitously complicated, the odds are good that it is documented below as the fix for a specific failure.

---

## 1. Why two functions

A serverless function can hold a WebSocket open, and a serverless function can run a loop. It cannot usefully do both for a shared room, for a reason that is structural rather than a platform limitation:

- The socket's lifetime belongs to **one client**. It ends when that client leaves.
- The authority's lifetime belongs to **the room**. It has to outlive any individual client.

Fusing them means the room's authority dies whenever one particular player closes their tab, and that twenty functions each holding one socket have no way to agree on who picked up the coin. So: **relay functions are per-socket and dumb, the ticker function is per-room and authoritative**, and Redis pub/sub is the bus between them.

This split has a happy consequence that is easy to miss. When the platform kills the ticker at its duration cap, **nobody disconnects**. The relays are a separate lifetime and keep holding their sockets, so from the client's side a ticker handoff is a sub-second gap in the snapshot stream rather than a dropped connection, a reconnect, a re-mint, or a re-anchor.

The relay's *own* duration cap is a different event, and it used to be a bad one. That function dies too, on the platform's schedule, so without anything further **every socket in the fleet was simply dropped and reconnected every ~13 minutes on a completely healthy deployment**: a visible gap, a re-mint, a fresh subscribe, a re-seeded roster, per player, forever. It is now a **warm swap**. The relay announces `relay-expiring` `RELAY_EXPIRY_LEAD_MS` (5s) before it closes, the client opens a replacement socket alongside the live one, and adopts it only once the replacement *delivers a snapshot*: a relay that accepted the upgrade and then failed its own bus subscription is open and useless, so "open" is not enough to swap on. Same room, same server timeline, same tick anchor, same clock offset, same buffered frames: a warm swap is deliberately **not** an epoch change, because an epoch change would throw away exactly the state that makes the swap invisible.

**The swap reuses the session already on hand, which makes an expired token its own failure mode**, and it is the one failure this mechanism makes *more* likely rather than less. A relay lifetime chain outlives a session whose `maxAgeS` is shorter than it, so past that age every replacement is refused (a 401 before the upgrade on Vercel, a 4001 after it on node), the swap is discarded, and the cap silently reverts to the cold reconnect the swap exists to remove, with nothing anywhere saying why. So a replacement that **closes or errors before delivering** marks the session for re-mint on the next connect, which gives the following lifetime cap a token that works. It is a flag rather than an immediate clear, and that is deliberate: the old socket is still open and still serving in that window, and both `frame()`'s held-pose room stamp and the own-pid `room-reject` guard read the session while it is. The flag is consumed in the one place the ladder decides what to dial next, immediately before the mint decision, so nothing a live socket depends on is pulled out from under it. A replacement that merely **misses its deadline** keeps it, deliberately: a slow answer is far more likely to be a cold relay start than a bad token, and throwing away a working session for that would re-mint on every slow start. Both outcomes count on `stats().swapsFailed`, which is the number that says a deployment is in this state at all.

**What the interpolation delay covers, stated by the arithmetic rather than by hope.** The delay adapts within 80 to 500ms and extrapolation continues for a further 150ms past the newest snapshot, so a client keeps rendering plausible motion across at most **650ms** of silence, and far less than that whenever the network has been calm enough for the adaptive delay to sit near its floor, which is the ordinary case. Past that, remote entities hold their last extrapolated pose until the stream resumes.

**And what happens when it does resume is a glide, not a resumption in place.** Extrapolating past the newest snapshot is a *guess*, and the moment real data lands the guess has to be paid back. On a clean handoff the world paused while `serverTime` kept running, so the entity was guessed forward and the truth is where it already was: the whole discrepancy used to be spent in one frame, measured at a **10.41 unit backward step at -625 u/s** on an entity whose true speed was 100. The difference is now an additive offset on the rendered pose that glides to zero over `EXTRAP_CAP_MS`, which turns that into **four backward frames of at most 1.16 units each**, and the offset is clamped to the displacement the extrapolator itself applied, so a real teleport or an authoritative correction still snaps exactly as it should.

**A cold reconnect holds the last poses** rather than blanking every remote entity from the moment the attempt begins until the new epoch's first snapshot. A stale pose is a better answer than no pose, and it is flagged `extrapolated` with a `speed` of 0 so a host that wants to fade or ghost them can.

**And the new epoch resumes FROM those poses rather than beside them**, which is the other half and was missing at first. Holding a pose across the gap and then rendering the first snapshot of the new epoch wherever it lands trades one blank for one jump: measured on a 350ms outage through a real socket, a **25 unit step at 1500 u/s** on an entity moving at 100, followed by five motionless frames while the buffer refilled. `RoomConnection.beginEpoch()` therefore calls `SnapshotInterpolator.resumeFrom(held)` immediately after `clear()` (the ordering is the whole thing, since `clear()` drops the seed too), and each held entity's first render of the new epoch begins at its held pose and glides onto the interpolated one over `EXTRAP_CAP_MS`. After: a worst step of **4.47 units**, a peak of 268 u/s, and no motionless frames. Confirmed again in a real browser across three server-side kills mid-rally, at four motionless frames per outage before the glide and a worst step of about **5 units**, with the entity count never dropping and no blank frames at all. The connection is the only component that can do this, because the held poses are `frame()`'s own output and the moment they stop being valid is the epoch boundary, and the interpolator can neither produce the first nor see the second.

**The clamp is the one place this differs from the extrapolation unwind below**, and for a reason: nothing was extrapolated here, so "hide only this module's own overshoot" would clamp to zero and refuse the glide outright. The bound is `RESUME_GLIDE_MAX_MS` (1000ms of the entity's own measured speed) instead, which is what keeps a respawn or a re-serve from being smeared across a second of screen time: in the browser run a goal landing inside a gap rendered as a fast glide to the re-served centre rather than a slide.

**And that speed has to arrive ON the seeded pose, which is a lesson about where state lives rather than a parameter detail.** The first version read it from the interpolator's own motion map, which is precisely the map `clear()` empties one line earlier in the documented call order. Every lookup therefore returned `undefined`, every bound was `Infinity`, and the clamp was **structurally dead on the only path anyone uses**: an entity that respawned 5000 units away while the socket was down rendered as a 4986 unit sweep at 33,000 u/s, in the method whose own docstring promised a bound. A value read from state the caller was just told to destroy is not a default, it is a guarantee that the branch never runs. The fix needs nothing from the host, because `InterpolatedEntity` already carries `speed`, so the map `frame()` handed back *is* a valid argument with the measurement already in it; the motion map stays as a genuine fallback for a host that seeds without clearing first.

### The two handoff budgets, which are not the same number

A **planned** handoff (the duration cap) is now a release, one 25ms lease poll, and a restore. The successor is a **standby**: it is spawned `standbyLeadMs` (3000) *before* the cap, so it has already paid its cold start and its `init` and is sitting on the poll at the instant the incumbent lets go. The exit spawn that used to do this job could not start until the predecessor had finished, which meant the room paid spawn plus cold start plus acquire plus restore, 0.5 to 1.2s, against a client that covers 650ms.

**And that budget is now measured on a real platform rather than derived from loopback.** On a Vercel deployment on 2026-09-03 (a personal team on the Pro plan, configured at `maxDurationS: 300`, which is the platform default rather than that team's cap, so the ticker handed off every 270s and the relay warm-swapped every 290s), a planned handoff reached the client as a snapshot arrival gap of **49 to 67ms** on a 50ms grid, with the server's own grid gap at exactly **50ms**: no tick was lost, the count and the timeline both continued, and the whole cost to a player was at most one extra frame of arrival jitter. The relay's warm swap at its own cap succeeded **twelve times out of twelve** across two runs of three clients, every retired socket closing 1005 clean about three seconds after its replacement was adopted, with **zero reconnects** in the second run. A cold spawn of the room's ticker to its first snapshot was **1.0s**, joining a room already running was **0.35 to 0.6s**, and the platform never killed a function at all, because the ticker exits itself 30s before the cap.

**Then the same room ran at the Pro cap, which is the configuration this document's own numbers assume.** At `maxDuration` 800 the ticker's `maxRunMs` is `min(700s, 800s - 30s)` = **700s** and the relay's `lifetimeMs` is **790s**, so a 27 minute run crosses two of each rather than five. Both planned handoffs were seen by all three clients, at 700s as an arrival gap of **50.0 to 50.1ms** and at 1397s as **66.7 to 83.4ms**, with the server grid gap exactly **50ms** both times; every client attempted two warm swaps and completed both, none failed, and the retired sockets closed 1005 clean at 788s and 1574s. Zero reconnects, zero stalls, zero terminals, mean rendered marker speed exactly 100 on all three. The client-side rendering in that run is the worst this project has measured (35 to 61 zero-motion frames per client, worst arrival gaps of 650 to 933ms, one backward step in 97,000 frames) and it is the **container** rather than the platform: three headless Chromiums inside Docker on a 16-core box already running a second three-client run. A starved renderer says nothing about a handoff, which is why the handoff numbers are the ones quoted.

**What none of those runs settled was the bus, and a probe has since settled that too.** Snapshot arrival gaps of 250 to 433ms landed about once per five client-minutes, which the interpolation delay absorbs at the price of roughly 200ms of remote-entity latency. The suspect was the managed Redis; it is not. The database is in the same region as the functions (`iad1`), and a probe running **inside** a function, with the browser, the relay and the last 80ms of network removed, measured PING at p50 1.26 to 1.46ms and p99 2.35 to 2.38, and PUBLISH to SUBSCRIBE at p50 1.28 to 1.66 and p99 2.10 to 2.32, over 601 and 2406 samples, with **no sample over 150ms in either run**. So the tail is downstream of Redis: the relay function (its subscriber's event loop, or the function being paused between snapshots) or the socket path to the browser. `relay.gaps` is the instrument that separates those two, section 13 has the reading rule, and the first run of it came back **empty**: over ten minutes with three clients the browsers reported eight arrival gaps over 250ms and the platform's runtime log carried not one `relay.gaps` line for that window, with other relay lines from the same deployment present in it. So the relay saw no bus gap over 150ms and no send lag over 50ms while those gaps were landing, which clears the ticker, the bus and the relay function and leaves the **socket path** between the function and the browser, or the client's own event loop. Those last two are not yet separated, because every arrival time here is inferred from rendered frames and a render stall reads as a gap; the measurement that would separate them timestamps socket `message` events independently of the render loop.

**Fluid compute can run the standby in the SAME container as the incumbent, and the handoff does not care.** The successor is spawned by an HTTP call to the ticker's own route, and the platform is free to land that call in a warm instance the incumbent is still running in. It costs the successor nothing, because the handoff is carried by the **lease** and the **checkpoint**, both of which live in Redis and are indifferent to which process holds them: the successor still polls, still restores, still continues the grid. The one thing it fools is an *observer*. A host that stamps an instance id at module scope publishes the identical id from both processes, so a genuine handoff is indistinguishable from no handoff at all, and on 2026-09-03 the platform's invocation log showed two handoffs where the clients could see one. Generate that id per invocation if you want the seam to be visible; nothing in the library needs it.

An **unplanned** death is a different budget and this document used to blur them. A crash or an instance kill runs none of that code, so recovery is the lease TTL, plus the relay's jittered poll noticing, plus a cold spawn: **5 to 7 seconds, measured**. The stall banner therefore *does* fire on every hard death, and the restored checkpoint may be up to `checkpointMs` old, which is a tick **regression** of up to 20 ticks rather than merely a gap. Closing that means shortening the lease TTL, which spends the split-brain margin the whole design rests on, so it is a measurement to make rather than a knob to turn.

**What that costs a player, end to end, on the defaults.** Measured through a real socket rather than derived from the parts: **4.7 seconds of silence**, the stall banner at **4.0s**, and a 17 to 18 tick regression that arrives as an **80 unit rewind in one frame**, then four backward frames and an 80ms hold. Both knobs are host options with defaults measured on the source game, so a deployment that wants a different trade can take it and this document will not pretend the defaults are the only sane point:

- `leaseTtlMs` / `leaseRenewMs` at **3000 / 1000** keeps the same 3x renew margin and cuts the silence floor to roughly **3.3s**. Below the banner's 4s threshold, which is the interesting part: the hard death stops being something the player is told about.
- `checkpointMs` at **250** cuts the regression from 17 ticks to **5**, at four times the checkpoint writes. That is Redis bandwidth, which section 3 explains is the bill.

Neither default moves on a host's behalf, because both spend something the defaults were chosen to buy: split-brain margin in the first case, bandwidth in the second.

What the design guarantees across a planned handoff of any length is that nothing structural happens: the socket stays open, the client's own prediction keeps running, the stall banner does not fire below `STALL_MS` (4s), and the successor **continues** the tick count rather than resetting it. "Continues" means the successor does not reset it, not that the number cannot go backwards: a restore from a checkpoint written up to `checkpointMs` ago starts from that tick, so an unplanned death can and does step it back.

**And the successor continues the TIMELINE as well as the count**, which is a separate fact and was missing until it was measured. `serverTime` is stamped from the tick grid, and a fresh process starting its grid at its own `Date.now()` puts that grid wherever the handoff happened to land: the timeline moved 20 to 40ms earlier per handoff, the successor's first snapshot sat 9 to 29ms after the predecessor's last while carrying a whole tick of motion, and the client, which is interpolating on exactly that axis, rendered **165 to 269 u/s for two or three frames** on an entity moving at 100. The same phase jump quietly eroded the client's stamping lead by half to eight tenths of a tick per handoff, never far enough to trip the re-anchor tolerance, and measured end to end at a server-side buffer depth of **5, then 4, then 1, then 0 over three handoffs**. So a checkpoint carries `gridAt`, the scheduled grid time of the tick it describes, and a successor continues at `gridAt + tickMs` when that lands inside an adoption window of one tick ahead of its own now and `GRID_CATCHUP_TICKS` (2) behind it. Both halves of that window are load-bearing: after a *hard* death the newest checkpoint is a periodic one the predecessor kept publishing past, and then the continued grid is far behind now and the window refuses it, so `serverTime` stays strictly increasing either way. Outside the window the local clock paces the grid and a constant `stampOffset` carries the timeline, so even a restarted grid cannot stamp behind the predecessor's last frame. Section 4 has the asymmetry, the offset and the timeline marker that covers the one exit which writes no checkpoint at all. The field is optional and additive, so it is a shape change and not a meaning change, and `CHECKPOINT_VERSION` did not move.

The ceiling is 500 rather than 250 because a stall LONGER than the ceiling cannot be covered by definition, so it snaps every time it recurs rather than once: the delay saturates and stops growing however many times the stall repeats. Measured on a repeating 450ms stall, a 250ms ceiling gave 26 frames above 300 units/second with a peak of 675 on an entity whose true speed was 100, while a 500ms ceiling absorbed the same profile completely (0 frames, peak 100.0). The ceiling is not a cost paid by everyone: the delay is measured from real jitter, so a clean connection settles near the 80ms floor and never approaches it. Going further, to the second or so that would cover a slow handoff outright, is deliberately NOT done: that is remote-entity lag charged to every player on a bad connection to hide an event that happens once per ticker lifetime.

**The rate the delay moves at is capped as well as shaped**, and that pairing is easy to get half right. The ease sets the *shape* of the approach; `DELAY_SLEW_MAX` (0.08 of wall time) sets its *rate ceiling*, for exactly the reason the clock offset is capped at 5%: both terms are subtracted from the playhead, so easing either one quickly is rendering every remote entity fast or slow for as long as the ease lasts. Without the cap, one 450ms hold cost **131 frames outside ±10% of true speed**. The constant is pinned from both sides: at 0.10 that same hold still costs 106 such frames, and at 0.06 a *repeating* 450ms stall costs 12 frames above 300 u/s. Only 0.08 reads zero on both, and both tests bind.

### Why Redis pub/sub specifically

The requirement is: deliver a message to N subscribers, at 20Hz, cheaply, from a process that may be replaced at any moment.

- **Pub/sub fan-out costs one command.** Measured: 5 subscribers, 1000 deliveries, still ~1 command per publish. Command count does not grow with room population.
- **It is lossy, and that is correct here.** A snapshot is a full state, not a delta, so a dropped one is superseded 50ms later. Durable queues offer at-least-once delivery, which is the wrong guarantee at the wrong price for a stream whose whole point is that the newest message obsoletes every older one.
- **A REST-style Redis API cannot subscribe.** The bus needs a real TCP connection. This rules out several managed "Redis-compatible" HTTP products for this specific job, though they remain fine for ordinary key reads.

Delivery-billed realtime services were priced and rejected: they charge per message per recipient, which is precisely the axis this design makes free.

**None of which makes Redis a requirement of the design, and `RedisLike` is where that is enforced rather than merely claimed.** Nothing above the seam imports ioredis: the ticker, the relay and admission all take a command client and a subscriber factory as arguments, and `src/server/redis.ts` plus the two adapters are the only files that know what a real one is. There are two shipped implementations of that interface now, which is what turns "swappable" from a statement about the types into a line a host can copy: `createMemoryRedis()` is three Maps and a pub/sub hub in one process's heap, handing back the same `{ redis, createSubscriber }` pair the Redis factories do. Import it from **`tickroom/server/memoryRedis`** rather than from `tickroom/server`, because the barrel loads ioredis at module top and a host choosing this shape has chosen not to. What the swap gives up is everything the bus was there for and nothing else: no horizontal scale (two instances are two disjoint sets of rooms with the same names), no survival of the process (the checkpoint lives in the heap it protects, which is fatal on serverless by definition), and a lease that is a re-entrancy guard rather than a split-brain guard. That makes it right for a single long-lived VM and for a local dev loop, and wrong everywhere else.

## 2. The lease, and the two-clock rule

Exactly one ticker may be authoritative for a room. The mechanism is a short-TTL Redis key:

- **Acquire:** `SET key owner PX 5000 NX`. Atomic, so a race resolves to one winner and the loser returns immediately without publishing a frame.
- **Renew:** a Lua script that refreshes **only if the value still equals our owner id**. Returns false the moment ownership is lost.
- **Release:** the same owner-checked pattern for delete, so a slow ticker cannot delete a successor's lease on the way out.

### The part that regressed twice

A ticker holds two timestamps, and keeping them separate is the entire safety property:

```ts
interface OwnershipClock {
  lastRenewAt: number;  // when a renew was last ATTEMPTED
  lastOwnedAt: number;  // when ownership was last CONFIRMED
}
```

Only a **confirmed** renew advances `lastOwnedAt`. An attempt moves `lastRenewAt` and nothing else. A renew that errors or hangs deliberately leaves `lastOwnedAt` exactly where it was.

**And a confirmation is dated from the ATTEMPT, never from the reply.** Redis extended the key when it *processed* the command, so crediting ownership from the moment the reply landed claims one reply delay more life than the key actually has. Measured with a 600ms delayed reply: **27 predecessor snapshots published after a successor legitimately held the lease**. The clock takes `max(lastOwnedAt, attemptAt)` so it stays non-decreasing when overlapping renews resolve out of order, which is the property the two-clock rule depends on.

**Nothing renewed between the acquire and the first iteration**, and setup is not short: `init` may instantiate wasm or boot a worker, the checkpoint read may retry, and `deserialize` rebuilds a whole world. An `init` plus restore longer than `leaseTtlMs` therefore lost the lease before the loop ran once; the ticker discovered it on its first publish, exited, and spawned a successor that paid the identical setup and lost it identically. A plain interval covers setup and stops the moment the loop takes over renewing, which is safe here precisely because setup is a sequence of awaits with no tick grid to stretch.

That interval has a seam of its own, at the moment it is cleared. A setup renew that replies `false` **after** the interval was cleared lands in the window between the setup lease check and the loop's first iteration, and setting only the setup flag there threw a *confirmed* loss away: the ticker would then publish into a room somebody else owns until its own renew cadence happened to notice, which with a long `leaseRenewMs` is not soon. It sets both flags, so whichever side of that window the reply arrives on, the run ends on the same fact.

**Every pre-loop exit runs the loop's own teardown**, which sounds like tidiness and is not. The abandoning paths (an `init` throw, a subscribe timeout, a checkpoint read that gave up, a setup throw, a setup lease loss) used to return on their own and leaked exactly the two things the loop's `finally` exists to release: the input subscriber stayed attached, which is one live Redis connection per abandoned run against a plan whose connection ceiling is the first wall this architecture hits, and `runtime.dispose` never ran, so a host holding a wasm instance or a worker leaked one of those per abandoned run too. They all go through one function now. None of them spawns a successor and none of them touches the crash counter, because none of them is evidence about the room's state.

Collapse these into one timestamp and the following happens. Renews start failing (a Redis fault, a latency excursion). The single clock is refreshed at every *attempt*, so the pre-publish guard never fires. The lease silently expires. A successor legitimately acquires it and starts publishing. **The original ticker keeps publishing too**, because as far as its own clock is concerned it is fine.

Now two divergent simulations interleave snapshots on one channel at 20Hz, and both write the same checkpoint. Clients see the world stutter between two realities. This is the worst failure mode the system has, and one collapsed timestamp is all it takes.

**And the margin those two clocks buy is measured now rather than argued, which is what this section owed.** The bound follows from three lines of code and not from this document's intuition: Redis extends the key from the moment it **processes** a renew, ownership is dated from the **attempt**, a publish requires `now - lastOwnedAt < leaseTtlMs`, and a successor's `SET NX` succeeds only once the key has expired. Put together: every predecessor snapshot is **issued before the successor can acquire**, and the renew round trip does not appear in that statement at all, for any `leaseTtlMs` above the command client's own `commandTimeout` (2000ms). `tests/splitbrain.redis.test.ts` measures it against a real Redis with the predecessor's command connection shaped by a TCP proxy, at a deliberately short 1500/400: a steady 50ms round trip, a steady 400ms one, and 1s spikes every third renew, each of them then black-holed. **No distribution lapsed the key on its own**, because renews are paced from the attempt, so Redis sees one every `leaseRenewMs` however long the reply takes; a lapse needs the path to die, and a dead path stops the predecessor issuing within four ticks anyway. Measured: the key lapsed 1466 to 1476ms after the path died, the successor's first frame landed at 1473 to 1493ms, and the predecessor's last snapshot was issued **469 to 1427ms before it** in every run. A path that heals after the TTL delivers what it was holding, at most `MAX_IN_FLIGHT_PUBLISHES` (4) frames, measured exactly four.

**The one case the round trip does reach is a theft rather than a lapse**, which is the Redis-restart case section 13 documents as open: the key vanishes under a live owner, so the lease clocks bound nothing, and the predecessor publishes until Redis refuses its next owner-checked write (the loop renew or the checkpoint) and it learns of the refusal one reply later. Overlap **40 to 260ms** at a 50ms round trip, 477 to 482ms at 400ms and 965 to 984ms under the 1s spike, bounded by `min(leaseRenewMs, checkpointMs) + RTT + two ticks`. Duplicate tick numbers after an unplanned death are the checkpoint regression section 3 describes rather than a second authority, measured at 0 to 10 ticks against a `checkpointMs` of ten ticks. So shortening `leaseTtlMs` to close the 5 to 7 second budget is still a trade, and it is now a trade with a number on it: what it spends is the lapse margin above, not the theft bound, which the TTL does not enter.

### The synchronous guard

Before every publish:

```ts
if (!mayPublish(clock, Date.now())) {
  owns = await renewLease(redis, key, owner);   // the ONE await outside the sleep
  if (!owns) break;
}
```

Three situations reach it. (a) The loop stalled past the TTL, typically a GC pause, so the async renew never ran. (b) Renews have been firing but failing, so the lease expired under us. (c) **The bus told us it is reconnecting.** ioredis queues everything issued while the socket is down, renews included, so the ownership clock is a statement about a connection that was not there; the guard runs on every iteration until one *awaited* renew confirms ownership over the restored connection, rather than waiting for `mayPublish` to age out on its own. In normal operation ownership is confirmed every renew interval, well inside the TTL, and none of this runs at all.

Whichever detector fires, **the exit names itself**: `ticker.lease-lost` carries `meta.finder`, one of `guard` (this synchronous check), `renew` (the async renew off the hot path), `checkpoint` (Redis refused an owner-checked write) or `setup` (a renew during setup came back false). Before that, a Redis restart that expired every lease in the fleet produced tickers exiting `'lease-lost'` with **not one line saying so**.

### Exit and handoff

```
ticker exits
  ├─ error exit  ──► NO checkpoint, NO successor
  │                  release lease (if still owns), incr room:{id}:crashes
  └─ any other   ──► final checkpoint (only if still owns)
                 ──► release lease   (only if still owns)
                 ──► finally: spawn successor if players remain,
                      unless a STANDBY was already spawned for a duration exit
```

The four exit reasons a caller can see are `'duration'`, `'empty'`, `'lease-lost'` and `'input-dead'`, plus `'busy'` (lost the acquire, did nothing) and `'error'`. `'input-dead'` is the newest and the one that needs explaining: the ticker's own liveness probe never came back through its subscriber, so it holds the lease over a room whose inputs it can no longer receive. It is handled exactly like a duration cap, because the subscription is opened once per run and only a fresh process repairs it, and reported separately because the two mean opposite things about the health of the deployment.

**A thrown ticker writes no checkpoint and spawns no successor**, which is the one case where "save what we have" is exactly the wrong instinct. The state at the moment of a throw is half-mutated by definition, so persisting it and spawning a successor hands the next invocation the very bytes that killed this one: it restores them, throws in the same place, saves them again, and spawns its own successor. **Measured before the fix: 40 spawns in 958ms**, a permanent crash loop with no ceiling and every metric reporting each successor as a healthy cold start. The last *periodic* checkpoint was written by a tick that completed, so the room falls back to a state that demonstrably simulates, at the cost of the seconds since. A counter in `room:{id}:crashes` (60s TTL) starts the room **fresh** after three consecutive crashes, which is the geometry-digest trade again: lose the in-progress state once instead of forever.

Four things about the ordinary successor spawn, each of which was measured:

1. **It is in `finally`, not at the end of `try`.** A room whose ticker died is exactly the moment it would otherwise sit dead. The `'error'` exit is the deliberate exception above.
2. **It is not gated on still owning the lease.** A lost lease is precisely the case where nobody else has been told to start. Firing blind is safe: a successor that already holds the lease fails its acquire and returns immediately.
3. **It is awaited, but raced against `EXIT_SPAWN_WAIT_MS` (3500).** Fire-and-forget was wrong: as the last thing the handler does, the request is post-response background IO the platform can suspend before flushing, and the spawn intermittently never left, blowing the handoff budget about 10% of the time. A bounded wait is required because the successor does not *respond* until it exits minutes later. **The bound has to outlast the host's own delivery receipt**, which is why it is a named constant rather than the `sleep(2000)` it used to be: the Vercel adapter decides delivery at `SPAWN_ACK_MS` (3000), so at 2000 the race was always decided first and the rejection could never be observed. That mattered because this spawn's rejection is the *only* line that ever says a room has no ticker on the `'lease-lost'`, `'input-dead'`, `'empty'` and refused-standby paths, where no standby preceded it. The 1500ms comes out of `TICKER_EXIT_MARGIN_MS` (30s), so the arithmetic clears by an order of magnitude, and the coupling is **pinned by a test rather than by an import** (`SPAWN_ACK_MS < EXIT_SPAWN_WAIT_MS`, with 500ms of margin), because a ticker that imports an adapter is a worse problem than the one being fixed.
4. **It is not `AbortSignal.timeout`.** Aborting could propagate as a cancellation and tear down the very ticker just started, which is far worse than the bug being fixed. Race a timer and leave the request alive.

**And what the spawn waits FOR is a delivery receipt, never an answer**, which is the same lesson one layer out and it cost a false error on every room start before it was learned. A ticker route does not respond until `runTicker` returns, because ending the response ends the function, so the answer to a spawn is the successor's entire life: 700 seconds at the default cap. Node's undici gives `fetch` a `headersTimeout` of 300000ms, so a spawn that awaits its answer **fails at 300s** with `TypeError: fetch failed` against a ticker that started 300 seconds earlier and is running the room perfectly. Measured on a real deployment, that reached the log as `relay.spawn-failed` on the first socket of every run, sitting beside the `ticker.restore` line of the very ticker it was reporting as failed, and as `ticker.spawn-failed` on every standby handoff. The Vercel adapter's spawns resolve on a receipt now (3 seconds, or the response if one arrives first, which a 403 from Deployment Protection or a lost lease race does) and reject only for a failure that lands *before* the receipt, which is the only kind that means no ticker was started. The connection is left open and only the wait is dropped, for the same reason as point 4: Vercel's request cancellation is opt-in per function path, and an abort at 3 seconds would kill a successor inside a cold start measured at 5 to 7 seconds on any project that switched it on. **The general rule: a request whose response is the callee's whole lifetime must be waited on for a receipt, and a timeout in that path is a statement about delivery rather than about health.**

**The standby is the fifth thing, and it is fire-and-forget for the opposite reason.** It goes out `standbyLeadMs` before the cap, while a whole loop is still running behind it to keep the process alive, so there is nothing to protect it from being suspended. A lead longer than the lifetime disables it rather than firing it on iteration one, because a room whose whole lifetime is shorter than a cold start has no planned handoff to smooth. On a long-lived host `spawnSuccessor` is a no-op for both kinds: looping back to the top of `for (;;)` *is* the handoff, and the process already paid its `init` and never gave it up.

**Three sizing rules the standby cannot be got right without.** First, `standbyMs` (how long the standby keeps polling before giving up) must comfortably exceed `standbyLeadMs` plus the incumbent's own exit, because the lease is released after the final checkpoint and the release, not at the cap itself: the adapters use 8000 against 3000. Second, it must fit *inside* `maxRunMs`, since a standby's poll is spent out of the same lifetime budget the platform is measuring. Third, and this is the one that reads as a bug when it is not: `standbySpawned` is set the moment the request is **issued** and cleared only if it is **refused**, never on resolve, because a spawn request to a ticker endpoint does not respond until the successor exits minutes later. A flag set on resolve would never fire, and the exit spawn it exists to suppress would fire beside the standby every time. A standby that was refused no longer suppresses the exit spawn either, which is the whole reason the flag is cleared rather than merely set.

**And the lifetime is measured from invocation entry, not from the acquire.** `maxRunMs` exists to keep the whole invocation inside a platform cap the platform itself measures from the moment the request arrived, so a standby that polled for eight seconds before winning the lease has eight fewer seconds to spend, not eight extra. Only the *ownership* clock is dated from the acquire attempt, and that is a different question about a different thing (see the two-clock rule above).

The relay's jittered lease poll remains as the backstop for *unplanned* death (a crash, an instance kill) where none of this code runs at all. **It now has a hold-off**, because a ticker cold start is longer than the poll period and every poll inside that window fired another invocation: measured at **41 invocations from 20 sockets during one 2.5s cold start**, all but the first existing only to lose the acquire race. The poll keeps running throughout, since it is the backstop for a spawn that genuinely never landed; what `spawnHoldoffMs` bounds is how often one relay is willing to *pay* for one.

The spawn token that authorises all of this is **room-bound and time-windowed**, not a one-shot. It rides the relay's outbound request as a `?k=` query parameter, which platform access logs persist well past the request, so anything that has ever seen the value can replay it. `makeSpawnToken` signs `roomId:window` over `SPAWN_TOKEN_WINDOW_MS` (5 minutes) and verification accepts the current window and the one immediately before it, so a token minted at a window edge is not refused a moment later. Two windows is the bound; widening it is exactly what turns a bounded leak back into an unbounded one.

## 3. Checkpoints

The ticker serialises the whole room every second to `room:{id}:state`.

**Gzipped.** It is the only large value written at a cadence, forever, per populated room, and it is repetitive structured data. Measured 3.9x to 7.0x on real payloads. Bandwidth is what a flat-rate Redis plan actually bills, so this is not micro-optimisation, it is the difference between a design being affordable and not.

**Encoded async, decoded sync.** The periodic write happens inside the tick loop, which may not block, so `zlib.gzip` runs on the threadpool and becomes the head of the fire-and-forget chain the write already was. Decode is synchronous because both read paths are already outside the hot loop.

**...and that is exactly why the writes are serialised through one chain.** Compressing before issuing the `SET` means two writes started in tick order reach Redis in **gzip-completion order**, and gzip time scales with the body. A periodic write of a large room still deflating when the *final* write of the same room starts therefore lands after it, and the checkpoint the successor restores is the older one. Forced and measured with an 8MB body, where the successor restored **tick 1**. The final write awaits the chain rather than racing it. Chaining costs nothing on the ordinary path (the writes are a second apart and the chain is long settled) and makes the ordering the room's rather than the compressor's, which is the only ordering that means anything.

**Compression failure falls back to plain JSON.** A checkpoint that was not written at all costs a room its entire state at the next handoff, which is far worse than a large write.

**Reads sniff the gzip magic bytes.** Not a stored flag, not try-JSON-and-catch. That is what makes a rolling deploy safe in both directions: while old and new instances coexist, a new reader transparently handles the plain JSON an old writer is still producing, with no migration step and no window where a room's state is unreadable to the instance that owns it.

**The read must be `getBuffer`, never `get`.** A utf8 decode of gzip bytes is lossy and destroys the payload before anything can sniff it.

**Decode throws on corruption, deliberately.** Every caller already wraps the read in a catch that treats an unreadable checkpoint as no checkpoint (start fresh). A throw therefore reaches the existing correct path rather than inventing a second one.

**But a thrown READ is not an absent checkpoint, and treating it as one costs the room everything.** A rejected `GET` is a fact about the connection; a body that will not decode is a fact about the bytes. Collapsing them meant one rejected GET produced `runtime.create`, a new incarnation, and a first loop iteration that *overwrote* the perfectly good checkpoint a millisecond later: **measured at tick 45 to tick 3**, incarnation changed, nothing recoverable afterwards. The read is retried three times and then the invocation gives up rather than starting fresh. Retrying costs a few hundred milliseconds of handoff on a transient fault; giving up costs one invocation and lets the relay's poll spawn a replacement that reads a checkpoint which is still there. Neither costs the room its state.

**The write is owner-checked in Redis, not gated on a local flag.** `owns` is a belief that lags reality by up to a renew period, so a ticker whose lease was taken while its last renew was in flight still thinks it owns the room, and a plain `SET` then overwrites the *successor's* fresher checkpoint with state from before the handoff. Measured against a real Redis at **three such overwrites inside 1.5 seconds**. `writeCheckpoint` takes an optional `CheckpointOwnerCheck` and runs a two-key Lua SET that Redis itself refuses once the lease has moved on, exactly as the renew and the release already do; a refusal *is* a lost lease and exits the loop rather than being retried.

**The TTL rides the same SET.** So it costs no extra command, a room with players can never expire (the write refreshes it every second), and a room nobody has touched in an hour is reaped and recreates from scratch on next join.

### The geometry digest, which is the subtlest thing here

A checkpoint carries **opaque state**. The ticker cannot tell whether that state describes the world the current deployment defines.

Without a guard, a deploy that moves a wall produces this: every live room restores its predecessor's bytes, keeps simulating the *old* world, and re-saves it. Each successor faithfully repeats this. The key's TTL is refreshed every second, so it never expires. **The room simulates a world that no longer exists, forever.** Players walk through new buildings and stop at removed ones. It is silent, permanent, and invisible in every metric.

The fix is one field. Stamp a digest of the world geometry and rules into the checkpoint; on restore, compare it; on mismatch, discard and start fresh. A geometry-changing deploy costs every room its in-progress state once, which is the correct price and is why such deploys belong outside peak hours.

The trap is that a hand-rolled digest only covers the fields somebody remembered to mix in. If you add a field to your world definition, add it to the digest, or the digest is quietly lying.

### The envelope version, which is the same failure through a different door

The envelope carries a version, and a checkpoint whose version is not the one this build implements is refused and the room starts fresh. The direction people expect to matter is the newer one. The dangerous one is the **older**, because it *parses*: every field is present and every type is right, so a body written by the previous version is restored in full and simulated happily even though the current build changed what one of those fields means. Same properties as the geometry mismatch, and the same fix. The comparison is `!== CHECKPOINT_VERSION` rather than `>`, and it runs **before** the field-type checks, so a version that dropped a field reads as the version change it is instead of as corruption. `inspectCheckpoint` returns *why* it refused (`absent`, `unparseable`, `malformed`, `version`) so the ticker can log it; an absent checkpoint is silent, because an ordinary cold room is not a refusal.

### The crash counter, which is the same shape a third time

The geometry digest and the envelope version both say "this stored state is not something this build should restore". The crash counter says "this stored state is not something *anything* should restore", and it is arrived at empirically rather than by comparison: three consecutive invocations that threw is evidence the checkpoint is the poison, whatever the fields say about themselves. All three have the same properties (silent, permanent, TTL refreshed by the very write that perpetuates it, healthy in every metric) and all three take the same escape: **lose the in-progress state once, loudly, instead of forever, silently.**

The counter's own TTL is short (60s) so crashes minutes apart are never mistaken for a loop, and getting that right is two details rather than one, both of which read as no-ops until the arithmetic is done.

**The window is FIXED, not sliding.** The `EXPIRE` runs only when the `INCRBY` came back 1, which dates the window from the *first* crash of a run. Setting it on every increment pushes the deadline out each time, so three completely unrelated throws 59 seconds apart would trip a limit that is supposed to mean three crashes inside one minute, and wipe a healthy room's checkpoint for it.

**And it is cleared by UPTIME, not by a checkpoint.** Clearing it at the first successful checkpoint sounds equivalent and is not: any poison that takes longer than `checkpointMs` to reach escapes the counter forever, because every invocation writes one checkpoint, clears the count, and *then* dies. Measured at four invocations with the counter stuck at 1. The clear waits until the invocation has itself run for a whole `CRASH_KEY_TTL_S` of uptime, which is a room that has demonstrably run rather than one that merely started.

## 4. The tick timeline

The naive scheme is "apply each input when it arrives". It produces two problems that look like netcode being hard and are actually just this:

- The same input lands on a **different tick for every client**, because every client has a different latency.
- It lands on a **different tick on replay** than it did live, so client-side prediction can never agree with the server.

### Stamped inputs and the playout buffer

A client stamps each input with the tick it should apply on. The server buffers it and applies it on **exactly that tick**. Now the server and the client's own prediction run the identical input on the identical tick, and prediction error collapses to approximately zero in clean conditions.

**"Exactly that tick" has one precise meaning, and it is pinned on `RoomRuntime.currentTick`: THE STEP THAT PRODUCES TICK T CONSUMES THE INPUTS STAMPED T.** `currentTick` is the number of *completed* ticks, the label the last snapshot carried, so the ticker consumes the stamp `currentTick + 1` and then steps, and the snapshot labelled T already reflects the input stamped T. That is what makes a client's reconciliation exact: it predicted record T as moving its entity during its own step T, and it replays its stored records from a snapshot applying exactly those with `targetTick > snap.tick`. The ticker used to consume `currentTick` *itself*, which applied the record stamped T to the step that produced the snapshot labelled T + 1. **Steady motion is identical either way**, because the record consumed a tick late carries the same value as the one that should have been, which is why no test and no harness saw it (the smoothness harness measures a server-driven marker, never the reconciled own entity). **Every input CHANGE is not**: measured on the shipped pong client, each paddle start or stop reconciled exactly one tick of travel out (4.5 units at 90 u/s and 20Hz), positive on the start and negative on the stop, at every RTT and with the replay window fully covering the lead, which the `ErrorOffset` turned into a visible wobble. The fix is the one that *removes* a tick of input latency rather than adding one, and the contract test changes the input mid-run, because a steady one cannot tell the two timelines apart.

`PlayoutBuffer<T>` is the data structure:

- **Push is out-of-order safe and duplicate-overwriting.** Packets may arrive in any order.
- **Consume is exact-tick.** Either the input for this tick is there, or the buffer starves.
- **Never-drop-late.** An input arriving for an already-consumed tick is *re-stamped forward* to the next consumable tick rather than dropped, unless a fresher entry already holds that slot (entries carry their original tick so a stale straggler cannot clobber a newer input). A late input applies a tick or two behind its intent, which is far better than vanishing.
- **Bounded ahead.** Past the ahead bound a push is *refused* rather than evicting what is already buffered, which is the opposite policy from a size bound and is the right one: evicting the oldest to make room means a sender stamping ever-further-future ticks destroys its own imminent legitimate inputs.
- **And a tolerance measured in ticks is not a tolerance, because the thing it bounds is a duration.** `PLAYOUT_MAX_AHEAD` (40) is two seconds at 20Hz and 667ms at 60Hz, so the identical constant meant three different things at three tick rates while the thing it is tolerating, how far ahead of the room a sender may legitimately stamp, did not change at all. The ticker's default is therefore two seconds of *this room's* ticks, `max(PLAYOUT_MAX_AHEAD, ceil(2000 / tickMs))`: 40 at 20Hz, byte-identical to before, and 120 at 60Hz. **The shipped client was never the one at risk**, and it is worth being exact about that rather than implying otherwise: the bound is measured from the consumed floor at ARRIVAL, and `RoomConnection`'s lead is RTT-compensated, so the round trip cancels and its stamps land within the lead margin plus the server-depth feedback of the floor, at most `PLAYOUT_MAX_AHEAD / 2` (20 ticks), at any rate and any latency. Who this is for is a THIRD-PARTY client of the documented wire, which may reasonably stamp a full round trip ahead of the server's current tick; under the old constant that client was refused sooner at 60Hz than at 20Hz for no reason it could see. Now the constant means the same two seconds everywhere. `playoutMaxAhead` still overrides it outright.
- **`RoomStats.refusedInputs` counts the too-far-AHEAD case and only that.** A sender whose clock runs behind is never refused: its stamps are late, they are applied at the next consume by the never-drop-late rule, and they show up on `lateInputs` instead. Keeping the two apart is the point of having both, because the fix differs completely: a rising `refusedInputs` is a configuration fault (this room's ahead bound is smaller than its senders' lead), while a rising `lateInputs` is a link.
- **A late push that lost a freshness check is not a refusal, and the return type says so.** `push` answers `PushResult`, one of `'kept'`, `'late'`, `'stale'` or `'refused'`. `'stale'` is the ordinary shape of a healthy redundancy window (a re-sent record whose slot a newer input already holds) and counting it as a refusal would make the one statistic that means "this sender's lead is past the bound" a function of the client's window size instead.
- **`lateCount` counts only the re-stamps that LAND.** A redundancy window re-sends already-consumed ticks constantly on a perfectly healthy link (3 per packet), so counting those made the statistic a function of the client's window size rather than of the link. And `RoomStats.lateInputs` reports the per-window *delta*, because `lateCount` only ever climbs and a link that was briefly bad in the first minute would otherwise look bad forever.

**The inbound window is pushed newest-first, but only after the buffer has consumed at least once**, and the two regimes are cleanly separated rather than traded off. Before the first consume, the first push is what establishes the reference the ahead bound is measured from, so newest-first would let one runaway far-future stamp set that reference itself and have the legitimate near-term records in the same window refused as too far *below* it, which is precisely the inversion the bound exists to prevent. After the first consume the reference is the consumer's own floor and no push can move it, so only the freshness question is live and newest-first is right.

### The starvation backstop

When the buffer starves, the obvious answer is repeat-last. That is right for **one** tick and wrong for a run of them.

Picture a player who turns and then straightens. Their packets drop. The server holds the stale *turn* input while the client drives straight. Divergence accumulates until the client's render correction adopts it, snapping the entity sideways. Then it happens again. This is the **directional sawtooth**, and it reads as the game shoving you.

So: repeat once, then from the second consecutive starve decay the held analogue value toward neutral by half each tick, snapping to zero near it. A starved buffer straightens out within about four ticks instead of holding a stale command indefinitely. `decayOnStarve` takes the streak **exactly as `onStarve` delivers it** (1 on the first starve); it used to expect the pre-increment count, so the natural wiring silently added one and decayed on the very first starve, against this module's own stated contract, and only the in-tree test knew to compensate.

**Early is not starved, and the backstop must not treat it as if it were.** A buffer that still holds entries has this player's inputs, stamped for ticks the room has not reached yet: nothing was lost, the two clocks simply disagree about where "now" is. That is the exact shape every handoff produces, because the client's counter kept advancing at wall-clock rate through a gap where the server's stood still. Running the streak there decays a held control toward neutral while the input that should be driving it sits in the buffer: **measured after a 600ms handoff at 16 starves and half a second of a held stick reading as released.** It is still reported as a starve, since the tick genuinely had nothing to apply, but always at streak 1, which is repeat-last. A genuinely *empty* buffer keeps the streak and the decay, which is what this section is about.

This is transport policy, not simulation. It lives outside the pure sim step, and the client deliberately does **not** mirror it (its rollback replays its own stored inputs), so a decay is a genuine divergence that the render error offset absorbs. Input redundancy makes it rare enough for that to be the right trade.

### Input redundancy: the cheapest win here

Every packet carries the **last few inputs**, not just the newest. Because push is duplicate-overwriting and out-of-order safe, the re-sends cost nothing but bytes, and a single lost or late packet can no longer starve the buffer, because the next packet carries the same tick again.

Packet loss stops being a visible correction and becomes nothing at all, for a handful of bytes per packet. Do this before reaching for anything more sophisticated.

### The snapshot's own timestamp, which is the client's playback axis

`serverTime` is stamped with **the scheduled grid time for this iteration**, captured before the loop advances it, never with a clock read taken after `runtime.tick`. Stamping it afterwards writes that tick's *compute time* into the playback timeline: a simulation whose step alternates 3ms and 28ms emits snapshots the client is told are 3ms and 28ms apart, so it plays a constant velocity back at **anywhere from 0.5x to 2.2x**. The tick grid is what the room actually simulates on and it is uniform by construction, so it is the honest label for the state the frame carries.

**The grid also has to survive a handoff, and a fresh process has no idea where it was.** Everything above is about the grid being uniform *within* one invocation; a successor that starts its own grid at `Date.now()` moves the axis by however far the handoff happened to land from a grid point, which the client renders as a burst on exactly the axis it interpolates on. That is why every checkpoint records `gridAt` and a restore continues from it. The arithmetic and the measurements are in section 1.

**The adoption window is asymmetric, and the two sides are different facts.** A candidate grid point *ahead* of now is simply not due yet, so the loop sleeps to it and one tick of slack is all that can ever be needed. *Behind* now is where a handoff actually lands: the successor has to acquire, read the checkpoint, `deserialize` and subscribe before it reaches the decision, and on a loaded machine that is routinely more than one tick after the predecessor released. A symmetric one-tick window turns exactly that case, the one the whole mechanism exists for, into the fallback it exists to avoid, and there is no middle answer available: the grid is either continued or restarted, so a loaded CI runner would render the same 165 to 269 u/s spike as a ticker with no `gridAt` at all. `GRID_CATCHUP_TICKS` is 2 behind against one tick ahead, and 2 is the bound the loop's own drift rule sets rather than a taste: the first iteration stamps the continued point and leaves the loop one or two ticks behind, the catch-up iterations sleep zero but still stamp exactly on the grid, and past two ticks the post-stall resync fires on the first iteration and abandons the grid after a single stamp anyway.

**Outside that window the local clock PACES the grid and a constant offset carries the TIMELINE.** Restarting the grid locally used to mean the first stamp was a bare `Date.now()` on a clock with no relationship to the one the predecessor stamped with, so a successor whose clock ran *behind* stamped its first snapshot before the predecessor's last: measured at 39ms backwards on a 40ms skew and two full seconds backwards on a 2s skew. A backward step on the playback axis is not a stall to ride out, it is a timeline that contradicts frames already in the client's buffer. Sleeping the skew off is not the fix either, since it hands the room a gap as long as the skew, which is the very thing the handoff exists to avoid. So the first stamp is the later of the two clocks' opinions and every stamp after it is the local grid plus that same constant `stampOffset`: continuous and strictly increasing by construction. A successor whose clock is *ahead* gets an offset of zero and an ordinary forward step, which the client already treats as a stall that resumed. Backward never happens at all.

### The timeline marker, which is the case `gridAt` cannot cover

A checkpoint's `gridAt` is the end of the room's timeline only when that checkpoint was the room's *final* write. A ticker that **threw** writes none (section 3), so the newest checkpoint standing is a periodic one the predecessor kept publishing past. A standby polling the lease every 25ms then wins it, adopts that grid point, and republishes a `serverTime` and `tick` pair the room has already emitted: measured at 3 of 3 stamps repeated.

So the error exit writes `room:{id}:timeline`, the last `serverTime` it stamped, **before** it releases the lease, because the instant the lease is free a standby can reach its own grid decision. A restore reads it best effort and applies it as a floor, `max(gridAt, timeline)`, whether or not a checkpoint restored at all: "no checkpoint to continue" and "no timeline to stay above" are different questions. Any exit that *does* write a final checkpoint deletes the marker, because that checkpoint's own `gridAt` is the grid point of the state a successor will actually restore, which is a strictly better answer than a floor, and a marker left behind by some earlier crashed invocation would keep raising the floor of every successor for a whole state TTL.

The crash counter that bounds the same path is written the same way: the `INCRBY` and its `EXPIRE` are **awaited inside the error exit**, raced against `CRASH_RECORD_TIMEOUT_MS` (500) exactly as the successor spawn is raced. Fire-and-forget, the `EXPIRE` is not even *issued* until the `INCRBY` replies, which is after `runTicker` has already returned, so a platform that freezes the instance the moment the handler resolves either loses the increment or lands the increment and drops the TTL. That is the crash-loop guard failing in both directions at once, from post-return background IO.

The same axis is why **the post-stall resync sleeps a full tick**. Setting the grid to `now` and falling through with no sleep ran the next iteration back to back with the current one, so two snapshots left with consecutive ticks and a `serverTime` gap of **0 to 1ms**: the client divides a tick's worth of movement by that gap and extrapolates at **5000 units a second**, which is the rubber-band the resync exists to prevent, produced by the resync itself. Sleeping to `now + tickMs` also hands the event loop the turn it needs to drain the envelopes that piled up during the stall, which is the other half of recovering from one.

### The monotonic client tick

The client keeps its own tick counter. It is anchored **once per connection epoch**, and thereafter advances by whole steps and never jumps.

**What it is anchored TO is `desiredTick()`, and that used to be a constant with no round-trip term in it at all.** The margin was a flat 4 ticks, which is 200ms of total budget at 20Hz and **67ms at 60Hz**, so every player above roughly 200ms of RTT stamped every input into a tick the server had already simulated, for the whole session, and the only symptom is input that feels heavier than the ping suggests. A lead that has to cover a *measured* round trip cannot be a constant expressed in ticks owned by a class that measures nothing, so `ClientTick.anchorTo` is now a bare `round(targetTick)` and the composition lives in `RoomConnection`:

```
desiredTick() = estimateServerTick()          // where the server is now
              + rttMs / tickMs                // measured, by the relay ping
              + ceil(inputLeadMs / tickMs)    // configured jitter headroom, default 150ms
              + feedbackTicks                 // optional, see below
```

**The default headroom is 150ms, and it was 100ms until the input timeline was corrected.** The ticker used to consume the input stamped T on the step that produced T + 1, which gave every input one free tick of arrival slack the lead was never sized for; consuming on the produced tick made the lead honest and the buffer one tick shallower at the same headroom. Measured on the Vercel deployment with three clients for three minutes at about 80ms round trip: `starves` 31 in 3593 ticks at 100ms after the fix, about ten a minute for the room, against about three a minute before it (33 in 11972 ticks over ten minutes). One more tick of headroom at 20Hz gives the same slack back at the same total latency the old timing had, with the input now landing on the tick it names, and the same run at 150ms reported `starves` 8 in 3610 ticks, `lateInputs` 2 and tick re-anchors of 0, 1 and 1 per client. The alternative, narrowing the feedback loop's deadband so the loop itself lifts a shallow buffer, was measured first and was worse; the buffer-health section below has those numbers.

**And 150 is a swept knee rather than a chosen number.** Three five-minute three-client runs against the deployment from a container with more jitter than a laptop (round trip minimum about 80ms, medians 87 to 99ms), one headroom each, about 6,000 room ticks apiece:

| `inputLeadMs` | `starves` | `lateInputs` | re-anchors per client |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 345 | 362 | 0, 0, 1 |
| **150** | **53** | **56** | 1, 1, 1 |
| 200 | 36 | 31 | 1, 1, 1 |

100 to 150 is a 6.5x cut, and the re-anchor column says why: at 100 the cushion is one tick after the timeline fix, and the feedback loop's two-tick deadband never lifts a buffer that shallow, so the loop sits out and the starves are simply paid. 150 to 200 buys another 1.5x for one more tick (50ms at 20Hz) of input latency on **every** action, which is the wrong trade for a good link and the right one for a bad one. So 150 is the default, and a host on a jittery path (mobile, a container, a satellite link) sets `inputLeadMs: 200`; a host that measures its own link lower sets it down.

**Re-anchoring is two-sided, and the ahead-only rule was measured wrong in both directions.** The old reasoning was that running behind is an ordinary latency spike the playout buffer absorbs with a never-drop-late re-stamp, so correcting it would trade an invisible re-stamp for a visible snap. That is true of a *spike* and false of everything that leaves the counter behind permanently: a routine 300 to 600ms handoff leaves the lead inflated by 6 to 12 ticks with nothing to correct it, and a frame longer than the render loop's own `dt` cap drops the missing time outright, **measured at 591 ticks behind fifteen seconds after a 30 second background**. So:

- More than half `PLAYOUT_MAX_AHEAD` **ahead** fires immediately, unchanged. That error is unbounded and every input sent while it stands is wasted.
- `REANCHOR_TOLERANCE_TICKS` (2) of error in **either** direction fires at most once per `REANCHOR_MIN_INTERVAL_MS` (2000). The rate limit is what keeps the two-sided rule from turning ordinary clock noise into a stream of corrections, and it is why the tolerance can be as tight as two ticks.
- The tolerance path is suppressed entirely while the caller's server clock is mid-step, which is the same lesson as the interpolator's suspended time prune: `desiredTick` is built from an estimate the caller has already stopped believing, so acting on it corrects the counter by the size of the clock step and the re-seed corrects it straight back. Measured on a handoff onto a successor whose clock ran 600ms ahead: **two opposite 12-tick snaps two seconds apart**, against none at all with the gate. The unbounded-ahead rule is deliberately *not* gated, because that one is a fact about the counter rather than about the clock and gets worse while it waits.

**A frozen render loop is an epoch boundary for the counter**, and not for the interpolator. Nothing was rendered during the gap, so there is no continuity to protect: the counter unanchors and the next snapshot re-anchors it, with `onTickReanchor` telling the host what moved. The interpolator's buffer and clock offset are still about the same live socket and are left alone.

**A stall short of that threshold is not drift, and the re-anchor decision does not read it as one.** The counter moves only in `frame()` while `desiredTick()` runs on the server clock, so a main-thread hitch of 100 to 300ms (a GC pause, one heavy frame) left the counter where the last frame put it and the snapshots that landed inside the hitch read it as 2 to 5 ticks behind: a spurious re-anchor forward, the resume frame stamping the stall's ticks on top, and a re-anchor back two seconds later, both of which every consumer's prediction paid for. `shouldReanchor` is therefore handed the counter **projected** by the time since the last frame (`tick.value + (now - lastFrameAt) / tickMs`, capped at `TICK_STEP_CAP` because that is all the resume frame will add, and only once a frame has run this epoch), which is exactly where the next frame will put it; a genuine drift moves `desired` by something that projection cannot explain and still fires. Every other reader of `tick.value`, the stamp above all, sees the raw counter.

**The counter also exposes how far it is into the next tick, because the predicted entity has no interpolator.** A locally predicted entity advances only when a tick is stamped, once per tick, while the page renders at the frame rate, so drawing its raw predicted state holds it still for several frames and then moves it a whole tick of travel at once: at 20Hz and 90 u/s that is a 4.5-unit step three times a second on the one entity the player is steering, and it was reported as stutter the moment the stamping fix took the rubber banding away and left it as the only visible defect. Remote entities never show it because the interpolator renders them between snapshots. `ClientTickView.fraction` (the accumulator over the tick interval, 0 inclusive to 1 exclusive, 0 after any anchor) is what lets the owned entity get the same treatment: `value - 1 + fraction` is a point one tick behind the newest stamp on the counter's own timeline, and `PredictedEntity` keeps the pose after every recent stamped tick so that point can be read off a real trajectory. **But it is read by a playhead, not followed directly, because a counter jump is not time passing.** Drawn as `prev + (curr - prev) * fraction` straight off the counter, a forward re-anchor of three ticks (the feedback loop's own answer to a starving buffer) stamped three records in one frame and moved the drawn pose three ticks of travel in 16ms, and a backward anchor of one (the ordinary epoch anchor on a link under 50ms) walked it a whole tick backward, neither counted as anything. The playhead moves through the pose history by each frame's own `dt`, within a tenth of real time either way (`RENDER_SLEW`), never backward, and past the newest stored pose only on a bounded speculation with the current input (the predicted entity, below), so a +3 is caught up over thirty ticks at ten percent extra speed and a -1 is lost over ten at ten percent under; a target further than `PLAYHEAD_SNAP_TICKS` (4) away is jumped to and counted on `stats.snaps`, which is what a frozen tab coming back or a handoff-sized re-anchor now is: one accounted snap. While the counter is unanchored (`frame()` unanchors it on a frozen render loop, then advances it by the clamped dt, and the next snapshot re-anchors it) the playhead runs at real time and neither chases nor snaps, so the resume and the re-anchor a frame later are one snap rather than two. A reconciliation moves every stored pose by the same delta, so the `ErrorOffset` alone carries the correction and the interpolation does not glide it a second time. The cost is one tick of visual delay on the owned entity, at most 50ms at 20Hz, on top of a prediction that has no round trip in it, and that is the right trade for an entity the player steers: the delay is not felt and the step was seen.

### The predicted entity, which is the shipped shape of the client half

Everything above about the owned entity is four coupled rules, and a host writing them out by hand has to get all four right at once: **stamp** one record per client tick (never per frame, never per keydown), predict each through the same pure step the server runs, and re-send the last few whole on every packet; **reconcile** every snapshot by replaying the stored records with `targetTick > snap.tick` from the authoritative pose, adopting the result in full and pushing the difference into an `ErrorOffset`; **snap** rather than glide on the first confirmation and when the offset would grow past a distance no glide can hide; and **draw** from a playhead that moves at real time through the recent poses, shifting the whole history by the same delta on every reconcile. Plus the re-anchor: a backward jump of the counter leaves a send high-water mark above `tick.value`, and a dedupe guard then goes silent until the counter climbs back past it, measured at 5.6 seconds of input silence and 100 self-inflicted starves on a real socket. The reference example carried two of the four wrong until the day before the helper existed, which is the whole argument for it.

`PredictedEntity` is that helper and it is **opinionated by design**: `conn`, the shared `step`, `maxSpeed` and an `initial` pose are the options, and nothing else is; the timestep is `conn.tick.tickMs`, read off the connection, because a `tickHz` option was the one number a consumer could get wrong against it. `advance(input, dt)` once per frame after `conn.frame()` stamps whatever ticks the counter crossed, sends the last `INPUT_WINDOW` (6) records as one JSON array of `{ targetTick, data }`, and returns the pose to draw; `reconcile(pose, snapTick)` once per snapshot replays and re-seats. The choices a host no longer makes: the replay history is `INPUT_HISTORY` (32) records, deeper than the six re-sent, because the lead over the newest snapshot exceeds six ticks on a slow link and a replay bounded by the re-send window came up a tick or two short on *every* snapshot there, a steady error the offset glided away as a wobble; the glide's per-frame cap is `maxSpeed * dt`, passed through `sample` from each frame's own delta rather than baked at 60fps, so the glide adds at most the entity's own top speed on top of its motion (a correction can briefly draw at twice it, never more); the snap distance is half a second of travel at `maxSpeed` and it is also the offset's cap, and the snap gate is on the offset an absorb WOULD PRODUCE rather than on the size of the one correction, so a run of corrections each inside the distance and together past it is snapped and counted rather than trimmed at the clamp in silence, which is what makes "the offset never silently trims" true; the correction is measured at the playhead (the drawn base before and after the history shift) rather than at the newest pose; a `step` result or an authoritative pose that is not a finite pose is refused as a counted snap (`stats.invalid`) rather than absorbed, because NaN compares false against every gate and once absorbed never left; heading interpolates by the shortest arc and reconciles by the wrapped difference; and a backward jump of the counter past one tick of slack is detected off `tick.value` inside `advance` and treated as what it is: **k ticks that have not happened yet on the server's timeline**. The prediction is rewound to the stored pose after `value - 1`, the mark moves there, and the records beyond it are dropped, because they are re-sent fresh as the counter climbs back and a playout push for a future tick the server already holds overwrites it; the poses beyond the mark stay as a speculative tail the playhead runs on through, overwritten in place as those ticks are re-stamped, and where a re-stamp differs from the tail (the input changed across the jump) the draw is measured at the playhead before and after and the difference goes to the offset, so it glides. Nothing is relabelled. The first shape relabelled the history and left the prediction where it was, so the next stamp stepped from the pose after the *old* newest tick and a -3 under a held key reconciled 22.5 units out until the dropped records aged out of the server's window. The tail alone could not keep the draw at real time: the next new stored pose is `k + 1` ticks of wall time away with under a tick of tail left, and a playhead held to the history's end spread that tail over the wait, measured at 0.11x for 8 frames on a -2 and 0.08x for 11 on a -3 at 60fps, a visible near pause on every tolerance correction. So the playhead is not held there: past the newest stored pose it draws a **speculation**, that pose stepped with the input the frame's `advance` was given, one tick at a time and at most `PLAYHEAD_SNAP_TICKS` deep, rebuilt on each frame that needs it and never stored, replayed or sent. The lost ticks are then paid the way a forward jump is paid, at `1 - RENDER_SLEW` until the target has caught the playhead up (about ten ticks per tick of jump, every frame at exactly nine tenths of the entity's speed, measured), and for a held key the speculation is what the re-stamps produce, so nothing steps; where a stamp or an input change makes it wrong, the draw is measured at the playhead before and after (against the speculation the previous frame drew, never the clamped history) and the difference glides through the offset. A jump past `PLAYHEAD_SNAP_TICKS` is one counted snap onto real history instead, and the same number bounds the speculation. `onTickReanchor` is telemetry now.

**There is a third call, and it exists because a glide is the wrong answer to an event the game already knows about.** `reconcile` cannot tell a respawn, a teleport or a round reset from an ordinary disagreement, so a respawn closer than the snap distance is absorbed like any other correction and the entity *slides* to its spawn over half a second. `snapTo(pose)` is how a host says it did not slide: the prediction, the pose history and the speculation are replaced by that pose at the current stamping mark, the offset is dropped, the jump is counted on `stats.snaps`, and the entity is **unconfirmed**, so the server's own answer for the same event snaps onto the truth exactly as a spawn's first snapshot does rather than gliding in from where the entity was before. The records are kept, because they name ticks the server has still to apply and the next reconcile still has to replay them; only the poses they produced are gone. Call it from the game's own event, off the snapshot that carries it, **before** `reconcile` for that snapshot, and never per frame: calling it on every snapshot throws away the glide that makes an ordinary disagreement invisible and draws the raw error as a jump each time. A non-finite pose is a `RangeError` rather than a counted refusal, because unlike a snapshot off a wire this is the host's own call with the host's own number in it. `examples/pong` does not use it, deliberately: no event in pong moves a paddle.

**The entity starts over at every connection epoch, and it is one per connection.** It watches `tick.anchored` from both `advance` and `reconcile` (the connection anchors and then hands the host the same snapshot, so an epoch's first reconcile lands before its first advance): a false-to-true edge drops the records, the poses, the mark, the playhead and the offset and unconfirms the entity, so the new epoch's first reconcile is a counted snap onto its truth rather than a 450ms glide from the old room's pose or a replay of thirty-two stale records against a count that restarted. A snapshot more than `INPUT_HISTORY` below every record held *inside* an epoch (a room whose count restarted while the connection's rate-limited re-anchor had not caught up) is a counted snap for the same reason. And because the ticker keeps **one playout buffer per pid**, a second entity on the same connection would overwrite the first's record for every tick it stamps and the server would consume whichever landed last: the entity is the player's input stream, a player steering several things carries them in one input record and one `step`, and constructing a second for a connection that already owns one throws. The one thing the helper still cannot do for a host is share the step: the record predicted with has to be the record the server applies, so `step` is the runtime's own function and `input` goes through the runtime's own clamp before it is passed in.

### The buffer-health seam, which exists now and is optional

**This section used to describe a step-interval dilation, within ±5%, driven by the server reporting how deep its playout buffer was running, and then to record that the control loop had been DELETED because nothing in the library could drive it.** Both halves of that history are still true and the feature is now built, in a different shape:

1. The ticker calls `RoomRuntime.onBufferHealth(state, pid, depth)` every tick it maintains a buffer for that player, on the **starved** path as well as the consumed one (a starve is exactly when the depth matters and exactly when `ackTick` does not fire), and once with 0 on the tick a buffer is dropped. The buffer lives inside the ticker, so this is the only route by which its depth can reach the host's state.
2. The **host** writes that depth into its own snapshot. Nothing in the library dictates the shape, which is why this needs no new control frame and no `TickerOptions` formatter.
3. The host's `decodeSnapshot` picks out its own pid's value and returns it as `DecodedSnapshotLike.inputLead`.
4. `RoomConnection` reads it as an EMA against a target depth of 2 ticks, corrects by at most two ticks and at most once per `REANCHOR_MIN_INTERVAL_MS`, and only when the depth is at least two ticks off target. A one-tick band was tried against a real deployment and measured worse (re-anchors went from 1 to between 4 and 8 per client per three minutes and starves rose, because every correction clears the client's stamped window and the loop hunts between depths of one and three), so the default cushion is set with `inputLeadMs` rather than by a nervous loop. The correction lands as `feedbackTicks` inside `desiredTick()`, so it arrives as one ordinary re-anchor rather than a silent drift.

**The client tick still does not dilate, and that is the design change rather than an omission.** The old seam steered the *rate* the counter advanced at, a control loop running on every rendered frame; this one steers the *lead* it is anchored with, a coarse correction a couple of times a minute against a quantity the server actually measured. `rttMs + inputLeadMs` is a good open-loop guess made entirely from the client's side of the wire; closing the loop converges the lead on the smallest one that keeps the buffer fed, which is the smallest input latency that player can have. Every step is optional: a host that never implements `onBufferHealth`, or has no room on its wire for the depth, simply never returns `inputLead`, the loop is inert, and the open-loop lead applies on its own.

## 5. Rendering other people

Remote entities are rendered **behind a deliberate delay**. This is the trade that buys smoothness and it is not optional.

**Playback runs on the SERVER's clock, not on the local arrival clock.** This is the one that decides whether any of the rest works. The server emits snapshots on a uniform grid; the network smears their arrival times, bunching several into a few milliseconds after a head-of-line stall clears and stretching the gap between others. Time playback against arrival stamps and that smear is replayed *as motion*: measured on an entity moving at a constant 100 u/s, arrival-clock playback rendered a peak of 1568 u/s, a standard deviation of 144, and 9 visible backward rewinds, where server-clock playback of the identical frames rendered 261, 20.7 and zero. The local arrival stamp is still required, for exactly one job: the minimum of `receivedAt - serverTime` over a sliding window is the least-contaminated estimate of the offset between the two clocks, and that offset is what turns a local `now` into a position on the server's timeline. It is eased toward that floor at no more than 5% of wall time, because the offset is subtracted from the playhead, so moving it IS moving playback in time, and moving it faster than a few percent of wall time is perceptible as remote entities briefly running fast or slow.

**A slow estimate needs an escape hatch.** A sliding minimum eased under a slew cap is right for noise and wrong for a *step*: a route change, a network switch, or a handoff onto a machine with its own clock skew moves the true offset in one jump, and easing toward a floor the window has not turned over yet strands the playhead off the buffer for the whole turnover. Measured: a permanent +1000ms one-way latency step cost 22.5 seconds of continuous extrapolation, rendering a 20Hz stop-go staircase. So a playhead that stays somewhere no buffered frame brackets for longer than `REANCHOR_AFTER_MS` (600ms) re-anchors the offset outright, **and only if enough frames are still arriving** (`REANCHOR_MIN_SAMPLES`, 5). That gate is the whole safety of it: an outage parks the playhead in exactly the same place and looks identical from inside the client, but there is no new data to anchor to, so extrapolate-then-hold remains correct there. The count matters as well as the fact: the re-anchor adopts the minimum of the samples that landed during the error window with no slew, so anchoring on a single straggler adopts that one packet's raw offset, which measured as a 600ms offset error held for twelve seconds. One visible correction in exchange for tens of seconds of degradation is the same trade `shouldReanchor` and `ErrorOffset.reset()` already make.

**There are TWO clock estimators in the client, and they are deliberately alike.** `SnapshotInterpolator` keeps one to place the playhead on the server's timeline; `RoomConnection` keeps its own to answer "what tick is the server on right now" for the stamping lead. Both are a sliding-window minimum of `localNow - serverTime`, both are eased under a slew cap (5%), and both have the same step escape for the same reason. Both also run on `performance.now()`: the connection's used to difference against `Date.now()` while every other timestamp in the client ran on the monotonic clock, so an ordinary wall-clock step (an NTP correction on wake, a phone picking up carrier time) **snapped the tick counter about 20 ticks** while remote rendering carried on untouched. `Date.now()` survives in exactly one place in the client, the version-skew reload window, where the question genuinely is wall time.

**Adaptive delay, 80 to 500ms**, sized to measured snapshot jitter and eased toward its target rather than snapped, under the wall-time rate cap described in section 1. A network that is behaving gets a tight delay; one that is not gets a looser one, automatically. The quantity it is sized from is how far each packet's one-way delay sat *above* that offset floor, which is what a jitter buffer actually has to cover. Sizing it from inter-arrival gaps instead is worse than merely imprecise: a delivery burst is a run of near-zero gaps, so it pulls the delay DOWN at exactly the moment more buffer is needed.

**On underrun, extrapolate. Never freeze.** Up to 150ms of velocity extrapolation. This is the rule most likely to be removed by someone who has not watched the alternative: a frozen entity that then teleports to catch up reads far worse than one that drifts slightly and is corrected. Freezing is never the right answer.

**And unwind the extrapolation as a glide, clamped to this module's own overshoot.** Extrapolating is a guess and the guess has to be paid back the moment real data lands; spending the whole discrepancy in one frame is a 10.41 unit rewind at -625 u/s on a clean handoff (see section 1). Two details make the glide honest rather than a smoother that lies. The offset may not exceed the displacement **the extrapolator itself applied** (its derived velocity times the time it ran, which the 150ms cap already bounds), so a real teleport, a respawn or an authoritative correction all arrive as a difference far larger than anything this module guessed and all still snap. And the difference is taken against **the guess projected one frame forward**, not against last frame's pose: the playhead moves between frames, so an entity moving normally is *supposed* to be somewhere else this frame, and differencing against where it was counts one ordinary frame of motion as overshoot. That measured as one frame at 0.0 u/s followed by nine at 109 on an entity moving at a constant 100, on a transition that was already smooth.

**There are TWO clamps in this module and they take their bounds from different places, deliberately.** The one above hides only this module's own overshoot, which is what makes a real teleport still snap. `resumeFrom`'s glide cannot use it, because nothing was extrapolated across a reconnect and "this module's own overshoot" is then zero, which would refuse the glide outright; its bound is `RESUME_GLIDE_MAX_MS` instead, and the speed it scales by has to arrive on the seeded pose rather than be read out of state `clear()` has already emptied. Section 1 has the mechanism, the measurements, and what that lookup cost while it was reading the wrong map.

**A rate measured against a clamped `dt` is not a rate.** `RoomConnection.frame()` clamps its frame delta to 0.25s so one stalled frame cannot advance a whole simulation at once, and passes the real `nowMs` alongside it. The playhead moves with `nowMs`, so a 30 second render gap moves playback 30 seconds while `dt` still says a quarter of one, and dividing the resulting displacement by `dt` reported **10,000+ u/s on an entity moving at 100**, for a dozen frames, on a tab that was simply in the background. `InterpolatedEntity.speed` is documented as the animation driver, so that is a sprint no entity performed. Past `FRAME_GAP_SLACK_MS` (100) of disagreement the smoother uses real elapsed local time instead; and a frame of zero length leaves the stored speed alone rather than overwriting it with zero, because rendering the same instant twice is not evidence that the entity stopped.

**Interpolate heading along the shortest arc.** Linear interpolation across the ±π wrap spins an entity the long way round, which looks like a bug because it is one.

**Derive animation from measured speed, not from a wire field.** A coarse enum on the wire can say "walking" or "running" and cannot describe an entity mid-acceleration. The client already knows the entity's real speed because it computed the interpolated motion; low-pass that and drive the animation from it. Better fidelity, zero wire cost.

### Corrections must never move the simulated pose

When the client's prediction diverges past a deadband, the simulation adopts the corrected state **in full and immediately**, because it is the truth. The equal-and-opposite delta is pushed into a render-layer `ErrorOffset`, so the **rendered** pose (simulated + offset) stays continuous through the jump. The offset then decays to zero at a capped per-frame velocity.

Net effect: the simulation is always on truth, and the player never sees a snap. A multi-second network dropout reconciles as a smooth glide over a second or two. The per-frame cap is what makes it a glide rather than a lurch, and it is why the cap belongs on the *step* rather than on the offset. `PredictedEntity` (section 4) is this rule applied to the owned entity, with the two deliberate exceptions built in: the first confirmation and an offset that would grow past half a second of travel snap, because gliding a spawn guess or a checkpoint rewind walks the entity in from nowhere, and a correction the cap would trim is a jump nothing counted.

## 6. Capacity, admission, and one source of truth

`room:{id}:stats` is written by the live ticker with a **5-second TTL**. That short TTL is doing real work: a room with no ticker reads as empty, so a dead room is automatically reusable.

**Both the relay's admission check and the balancer read that same key.** This is load-bearing.

If the relay read the persistent metadata hash instead, a ticker that died hard while players were present would leave that hash phantom-full forever. The balancer, reading stats, would see the room as empty and keep sending joiners to it. The relay, reading meta, would keep rejecting them. The client's bounded re-assign loop would exhaust itself and strand every joiner on "full", **with no path to self-heal**. The room is bricked for new players and nothing in the system can notice.

One key for capacity. The metadata hash is consulted only to recognise a *rejoin*, which it can still do correctly while stale.

Admission fails **open** on a read error: a monitoring failure must never become an outage. What it must not do is fail open *quietly*. A Redis fault disables the per-user socket cap in exactly the conditions the cap is load-bearing, so `checkAdmission` reads the pipeline's per-command errors and reports `socketCapEvaluated: false` rather than letting a degraded read look like a passing check.

**The whole sequence is one function, `admitSocket`, and it lives in `src/server/`.** Check, warn on a cap that could not be evaluated, refuse with the right frame and close code, register the connection, attach the relay, unregister on close. It used to be written out in `adapters/vercel.ts`, imported from there by `adapters/node.ts` (one platform's adapter as another's dependency, for a decision that is not platform-specific), and hand-copied into the node example: three copies of a protocol whose close codes the **client** latches a terminal reconnect state off, free to drift into "the same" protocol answering differently per host, with nothing in any gate able to see it. Registration is paired with the relay here rather than left to the host because it was left to the host and all three got it wrong the same way: they called `checkAdmission`, never registered anything, and `zcard` counted an empty set forever while the cap looked like it was working.

**Pairing the registration with the relay means pairing it with the relay's THROW as well as its close.** The `ZADD` is already written by the time `attachRelay` is called, so a throw out of it leaves a member nobody will ever `ZREM`: it ages out only after `connStaleMs` and until then it counts against the cap for a socket that never existed. It is not a theoretical throw either, it is the first-deploy one, since `createSubscriber` throws synchronously when `REDIS_URL` is unset, so a misconfigured deployment burned a cap slot on every attempt while every attempt also failed. The registration is unwound on the throw path as well as on `onClose`.

There is a boundary race (two joiners can both see the last slot), so the ticker enforces the cap authoritatively and publishes a `room-reject` for the loser. **That frame had no consumer, which is the other half of this and was missing for a long time.** It goes out on the roster channel, which is a broadcast, so forwarding it verbatim sent a rejection for one pid to every socket in the room, re-sent it on every heartbeat, and had every client ignore it, while the player it was actually meant for stayed connected forever receiving snapshots with no entity in them and no idea why. **The relay is the only place that knows which socket carries which pid**, so it is the only place the frame can be aimed: the relay whose pid matches sends `room-full`, closes with `CLOSE_CODES.capacity` and stops its join heartbeat, and every other relay drops the frame.

**And the pid is not a fine enough aim.** A pid deliberately holds two live sockets at a time, during a relay warm swap and during the overlap of an ordinary reconnect, and section 9 is about how routine that is. A pid-only reject closed **both** of them, measured at two 4002s for one refusal, which turns a capacity bounce into a dropped session. So the ticker carries the refused join's own connection id `c` on the reject, the same `c` the relay already stamps on every join and leave, and a relay matches on it. A reject arriving *without* `c` still matches on pid alone, because that is an older ticker and there is nothing more precise available to match with. The client latches `'capacity'` **and closes the socket**, and a host completes the loop with a bounded re-assign: `onTerminal: (r) => { if (r === 'capacity' && tries++ < 3) void conn.start({ remint: true }); }`, where the re-mint is what lets the host consult its balancer with **every** room it has been refused from excluded, not merely the last one. The relay pre-check is the fast, friendly path, not the guarantee.

### Multi-room

A base room id holds up to 50 instances: `lobby` is instance 0, `lobby~1` and up are the rest. Instance 0 keeping the bare name is deliberate, so adding multi-room to a live deployment migrates no existing Redis state.

The balancer packs into the **lowest-index room with space**, so rooms fill in order and empty ones drain rather than leaving players scattered one per room.

**A client can now exclude more than one instance, and that changes which branches are reachable rather than only how many ids fit.** `BalancerOptions.exclude` takes a `string | string[] | null` and the Vercel route parses `?not=a,b` into it, up to `MAX_EXCLUDE_IDS` (64) with the excess dropped rather than answered with a 400, because a client that has been bounced a few times is already having a bad enough time without its re-assign turning into an error. Each entry is validated on its own against the base, so a stale id carried over from a previous session is ignored without taking the good entries down with it. The consequence worth writing down is that **"every instance in the pool is excluded" is now an ordinary outcome** of a client that has walked the whole pool, where it used to be unreachable with only one id ever excluded. The route answers it the way it answers a genuinely full pool: it hands back an excluded room rather than manufacturing a refusal, because a room that was measured and full is a fact and "there is nowhere left that you have not already been" is not a capacity reading. Two branches in `assignRoom` exist for exactly that case, the `mget` failure path and the every-candidate-full path, and both of them were written when it could not happen.

`normalizeRoomId` is a **trust boundary**: the raw value comes from a query parameter, and it is interpolated into Redis key names, which have no escaping. Anything that does not validate falls back to a known-good id. Note in particular that a bare `raw in WORLDS` check on an object literal matches **inherited** properties, so `constructor`, `__proto__` and `toString` all pass. Use `hasOwnProperty`.

**And the fallback that makes it safe is also the thing that hides a misconfiguration, so it says so now.** `maxRooms` is three independent options (the ticker route, the relay route, the balancer route), each defaulting to `MAX_ROOMS_PER_BASE` (50), and **all three must agree**. A balancer at 50 against a relay at 4 hands a client `lobby~7`, which the relay refuses as out of range and replaces with the fallback: the session says one room, the snapshots come from another, and every signal on both ends reads healthy. The socket is open, the roster is populated, the tick rate is nominal, and the player is simply in a game nobody else can see them in, with nothing in the system able to notice because from each component's own point of view nothing went wrong. So both routes and `attachNodeRelay` log one `ticker.room-normalised` / `relay.room-normalised` warn per request when a non-empty `?room=` differs from the id that came out, carrying the raw value truncated to 64 characters. It sits **behind the token check** on all three, because a log line per request is a log-volume amplifier until the requests are authenticated. That line is the tell, and it is the only one there is.

## 7. Backpressure and abuse

Four bounds, at four layers, each catching something the others cannot.

**Per-socket token bucket** (capacity 100, refill 70/s, sized against a client ticking up to 60Hz plus headroom, not against a flood). Burst tolerance with a sustained cap. Excess frames are dropped silently, exactly as a lossy network drops them, which the input redundancy window is already designed to survive.

**Per-sender inbox quota.** A global inbox cap alone is a shared resource one sender can monopolise: a single flooder fills the queue and *everyone else's* inputs are shed at the door, degrading the room for all twenty rather than just the abuser. The per-sender quota sheds the flooder's own newest, keeping the cost where it belongs. `20 × perSenderCap` (64) stays well under the global cap (4096) so the per-sender bound binds first in the single-abuser case, which is the point.

**It is a backstop, and a socket-borne flooder does not reach it.** Measured with twenty clients for twenty seconds, one of them sending 200 frames a second: `RoomStats.dropped` stayed **0 in all 44 stats flushes**, because the excess dies one layer up at that socket's own token bucket (3242 of 3777 frames dropped, capacity 40 refill 25, the defaults at the time and now 100 and 70 in `relay.ts`, giving a measured 26.8 per second through the door) and 25 envelopes a second against a per-tick drain never accumulates 64 undrained. What the quota is actually for is a producer that reaches `room:{id}:in` **without a relay in front of it** (another deployment on the same Redis instance, a script, a compromised host), and a loop that stops draining for seconds at a time. Reading a `dropped` of 0 as "the quota is not working" is exactly backwards: on this path it is the token bucket reporting first.

**Per-user socket cap.** The token bucket is per *socket*, so without this one client simply opens more sockets to multiply its allowance. Worse: **every socket holds its own Redis subscriber connection**, and managed Redis caps concurrent connections per database. Enough sockets from one client exhausts that ceiling and takes the **room ticker's** subscriber down with it. That is a total outage, not a nuisance, and it is the real reason this cap exists.

**Snapshot backpressure, on the way OUT.** The three bounds above are all inbound; this one is the only place the library could turn a deliberately lossy bus into a reliable unbounded queue, and doing so is strictly worse than dropping. Redis pub/sub already drops a message with no subscriber, the client's interpolator is built to survive gaps, and the input redundancy window exists because loss is ordinary. Forwarding regardless gave a paused or backpressured socket a queue that grew without limit (**measured at 7.65MB for one paused socket**, by pausing the `ws` stream itself, which is the shape a wedged transport has) and then replayed it as a stale burst the moment it drained, handing a jitter buffer several seconds of the past at once. Past `snapshotBacklogBytes` (32768) of `bufferedAmount` the frame is dropped and counted; the newest snapshot supersedes every older one, so it costs nothing the next tick does not repair. **Control frames on `metaout` are not subject to this**, because a roster change is announced exactly once and nothing repeats it: dropping one is permanent where dropping a snapshot is not. The same shape bit the ticker from the other end, since ioredis buffers commands across a reconnect and replays them (one blip inflated every client's delay to ~470ms for six seconds), so its publishes are bounded too, at four in flight, with the rest counted on `RoomStats.publishSkipped`.

**What this bound is, precisely: memory safety against a wedged socket, not staleness control.** It keys on `bufferedAmount`, which is the transport's own *userspace* queue, and a modern kernel absorbs a stalled reader into its send buffer long before that queue grows: macOS auto-tunes to about 4MB, so an 8 second read stall of 134KB of snapshots left `bufferedAmount` at **0** and fired no drop at all. A slow reader therefore gets the snapshots, late, and the client renders the documented catch-up when it resumes: measured on a 2 second stall, 242 motionless frames during it and then one frame of the stall's whole motion at 10,000 u/s. That is the "resume after more than 650ms of silence" snap section 1 already documents, not a stale-burst artefact, and it is the correct outcome, because playback is on the server timeline and the frames carry their own honest stamps. Do not read a `backlogDrops` of 0 as the bound not working; read it as the kernel not having handed the transport a queue to grow.

**Refusals must be cheap.** Anything whose rate a client controls is *counted in process* and flushed on a cadence the client cannot drive, never written to Redis on the request that triggered it. Otherwise a rejected request costs more than an accepted one and the rate limiter becomes an amplifier. The same rule applies to logging: a per-message log line on a client-controlled path hands an abuser a log-volume attack. **The ticker was the one place that had forgotten it**: a malformed input envelope wrote a warn line per envelope, measured at **51 lines for 51 `{data:null}` inputs**, through this library's own default decoder. It is counted now (`hostErrors`, `unknownEnvelopes`) and summarised once per stats flush, silent at zero so a healthy room says nothing.

## 8. Socket liveness

A half-open socket (network dropped, laptop slept, tab died without a close frame) is **indistinguishable from a quiet one** at the server: nothing arrives, and nothing errors. Left alone, the relay keeps publishing that player's join into the room and holding resources until the function's duration cap.

- **Any inbound frame is proof of life**, including one the rate limiter is about to drop (it still proves the peer is there) and including a protocol pong.
- **A ping rides the existing presence timer**, so liveness costs no extra timer and no extra Redis command.
- **Past the deadline, `terminate()`, not `close()`.** A peer that is already gone never answers the closing handshake, so a graceful close keeps everything running until the ws close timeout expires. `terminate` emits `close` immediately and runs the normal cleanup.
- **...and then cleanup runs anyway, rather than waiting for that event.** `terminate()` on a socket that has *already* closed returns without emitting anything at all, so the transport's own event is not something the loop may depend on. `cleanup` is idempotent, so a transport that does emit costs nothing for the belt.
- **A missing pong alone never closes anything.** Only total inbound silence does. But plain inbound recency is not a safe fallback on its own, and the deadline is sized for that: Chromium throttles a hidden tab to about one timer callback a minute, and measured on a real backgrounded tab it did so from the **second** minute rather than the fifth, so a client pinging every 2s sends one frame a minute and `requestAnimationFrame` stops entirely. At the old 45s deadline that reaped every backgrounded tab, on a healthy socket. The default is now **90s**, above one throttled interval with slack, and must stay above 60s for any browser client. The protocol pong is what makes a shorter deadline safe, because a browser's network stack answers it below page JavaScript; it is optional (`RelaySocket.ping`), so a relay whose transport has none logs `relay.no-ping` once at attach and reports `RelayHandle.transportPings: false`.

The pong path is what covers a **backgrounded tab**, whose timers the browser throttles and whose animation frames stop entirely. Pongs are answered by the browser's network stack, below page JavaScript.

**What a throttled tab still does well, and where it does not**, because the two are easy to collapse into "hidden tabs are broken". A relay's own lifetime cap is fine there: the swap is **message-driven**, `relay-expiring` arrives on the socket, and an incoming message is not something Chromium throttles, so a backgrounded tab opens its replacement and adopts it inside the 5s lead exactly as a foreground one does. Measured on a genuinely dark tab for 6.5 minutes on 2026-09-03: one relay warm swap and one ticker handoff both crossed while hidden, the swap succeeded, its retired socket closed 1005, the connection stayed open with **zero reconnects** and the player was still in the roster on return, at 46 pings for the whole run against a 90s liveness deadline that the old 45s would have failed. Showing the tab again drew the first frame and re-seated the player at **1.05s**. **Safari is measured now too, and it throttles far less**, which is the safe direction for a deadline sized against Chromium: the real Safari.app 26.6.2 over `safaridriver`, 6.5 minutes, sent **93 pings while hidden**, about one every four seconds against Chromium's one a minute, with the socket open throughout, zero reconnects, no terminals, still in the roster on return, and recovery at **1.007s**. So the 90s default is never approached there. **Read that with its method caveat**, because it is a caveat about how the tab was observed rather than about what it did: WebDriver would not report a background tab's state while another tab sat in front of it, so the harness switched to the measured tab for each 30s sample and back, making it briefly visible thirteen times. It is a tab hidden thirty seconds at a time, not one hidden for 6.5 minutes straight, and a read without that switch (a `BroadcastChannel` to a visible helper tab, or the page posting its state to the server) is still owed, as is mobile. The residual is the failure path, and it is still unmeasured. When a swap fails the client falls back to the reconnect ladder, and that is a **timer**, which the throttle does reach: a retry that would have run in 100ms can wait out the throttled interval instead, stretching the outage to as much as a minute. That is a good deal narrower than "nothing works while hidden", and it is now the only part of this that nobody has watched.

**A socket can be dead before the relay ever sees it.** The caller awaits `checkAdmission` to get here, so the socket may have closed during that round trip with its `close` event already fired and gone; attaching anyway produced a permanent zombie holding a slot against the per-subject cap until the function's own duration cap. `attachRelay` therefore checks `readyState` first: CLOSING or CLOSED runs cleanup at once and subscribes nothing, and **CONNECTING is deferred rather than skipped**. An upgrade callback can fire while the handshake is still completing, and `ws` throws on a send in that state, so the first join publish, the roster seed and the timers all wait for `'open'`; the subscribe still goes out immediately, and any snapshot arriving before OPEN is dropped by the ordinary backpressure path rather than sent.

**A deferral needs a deadline, because `'open'` is not guaranteed.** Waiting for an event that may never arrive is the same trap as waiting for `terminate()`'s close event, one paragraph up, and it costs more: a socket stuck in CONNECTING held its subscriber connection, its slot against the per-subject cap, and its heartbeat timer until the function's own duration cap. The CONNECTING path arms an open deadline (`subscribeTimeoutMs`) alongside the listener and cleans up on `relay.open-timeout`.

**And the control frames produced in that window are queued, not attempted and not dropped.** Attempting them throws, which used to write one `relay.send-failed` line per frame for a socket that had done nothing wrong; dropping them loses a roster seed, which is announced once and repeated by nothing (see section 9). So they go into a small queue (newest 8) that flushes on open, `seedRoster` goes through the same path, and nothing is logged per frame.

**A frame queued before OPEN and then closed is dropped, and the CLOSE CODE carries the meaning.** The queue is for a frame that has to survive a slow handshake and still be there when the wire comes up: a roster seed, an expiry announcement. It is not a delivery guarantee for a frame that *accompanies a close*, and one real case hits that, a `room-reject` landing for a socket still CONNECTING: it queues its `room-full` and then closes, `cleanup` sets `closed`, and the flush never runs. That costs nothing, because `CLOSE_CODES.capacity` is what the client latches its terminal state off and the frame is only the human-readable half. Flushing on the way out would mean sending on a socket the transport has just been told to close, which `ws` answers with a throw. If a frame ever carries meaning its close code does not, it needs a delivery path of its own rather than a wider queue.

**`refuseSocket` needed the same treatment from the other side, and it needs TWO latches rather than one.** It registers its refusal attempt on `'open'` as well as trying immediately and retrying at 250ms, because a handshake that completed at 300ms was never refused at all. And `frameSent` and `socketClosed` are separate flags on purpose: a single "already refused" flag set before the send, or set by the catch, makes a **throwing** send look like a completed refusal. Measured on a socket whose `send` throws, the close codes after 320ms were `[]`: the connection stayed open, unrefused and with no relay attached to it, through both the immediate attempt and the `'open'` listener, while the shipped test asserted only `not.toThrow()`. The frame is best effort; the close *is* the refusal, and nothing a send does may cancel it. The 250ms backstop therefore closes whenever the socket is not already closed, rather than whenever the frame did not go out.

**A run of failing sends is a dead socket; one failing send is not.** Send failures on an OPEN socket are counted and flushed once per heartbeat (`relay.send-failed` carrying a `count`), on exactly the rule the rate limiter and the ticker's own summaries follow: a client can drive that path, so a line per occurrence is a log amplifier. Three consecutive throwing sends terminate the socket and run cleanup, because at that point the transport has said the same thing three times and holding the slot costs the room.

**A subscriber that dies is not a subscriber that errors loudly**, and both events arrive on a connection the client can never see. A relay that ignores them keeps an open socket that will never receive another snapshot, so the player's own stall detector eventually shows a banner, which is a far worse answer than closing. The subscribe is therefore raced against `subscribeTimeoutMs` (5000) and a dead subscriber closes the socket, both with `CLOSE_CODES.relayUnavailable` (4004). **That code is deliberately not terminal**: the client's ordinary reconnect ladder answers it and lands on a fresh relay with a fresh subscriber. It is also what the relay's own `lifetimeMs` closes with, after announcing `relay-expiring` five seconds ahead.

### The relay probes its own subscription, for the reason the ticker does

A subscriber that is **black-holed after a successful subscribe** produces none of those events. A dropped NAT mapping or a firewall discarding packets with no FIN and no RST leaves the connection OPEN as far as both ends are concerned, so ioredis never reconnects, `'end'` never fires, the resubscribe never happens, and the inbound liveness check measures only what the *client* sends, which a perfectly healthy chatty player keeps doing. Measured against real ioredis behind a black-holing proxy: **zero frames, no log line, no close, forever**, bounded only by `lifetimeMs` where a host sets one and by nothing at all on the node adapter. This is the ticker's own black-hole problem (section 13) on the other side of the bus, and it takes the same answer, because the only way to learn that a channel still delivers is to send something down it and watch for it coming back.

So the relay subscribes to a **third channel of its own**, `{ns}:{roomId}:relay:{conn}`, and publishes one probe on it per heartbeat per socket. `PROBE_MISS_LIMIT` (3) unanswered probes plus the beat that notices logs `relay.subscriber-dead` once, terminates the socket and runs cleanup with 4004, so the client's ordinary ladder lands it on a fresh relay. It is paced by the heartbeat rather than by a knob of its own, which is the lesson `ticker.ts` learned the expensive way: give liveness a second independent period and somebody eventually derives it from a metrics setting. The bill is one extra `PUBLISH` per socket per second, roughly 5% of the ~21 commands per player per second in the README's cost model.

**Per connection rather than per room, and that is the whole design of it.** A channel shared by a room's sockets is quadratic in room size on delivery, and worse, it lets a *healthy* subscriber answer for a dead one, which turns the one signal built to catch this into the thing that hides it. It is the same reasoning as the owner filter on the ticker's probe.

**And an answer is bounded by what was asked.** The channel name contains `conn`, which this relay publishes in the clear on every join envelope, so anyone who can write to the bus can address it. A single forged `{ t: 'probe', n: 1e15 }` against a merely-monotonic check pins the answered counter above every `n` this relay will ever send, so `sent - answered` can never reach the limit again and the watchdog is dead for the socket's lifetime: measured, the control terminated with one line and the poisoned one never terminated across ten seconds of heartbeats. The check is `Number.isInteger(n) && n > answered && n <= sent`, on both this probe and the ticker's, which caps a forgery at the current beat. A reply to a question nobody asked is not an answer.

### A relay forwards to its socket only the frames addressed to it

The roster channel is a **broadcast**, and four of the library's own `SERVER_FRAMES` are per-socket facts that only the relay holding that socket may originate: `room-full`, `conn-limit`, `relay-expiring` and `pong`. Forwarding them verbatim meant one `{ t: 'room-full' }` reaching that channel latched **every client in the room** into a terminal capacity state and closed their sockets, measured on an innocent client from a single frame. So the roster channel has an allowlist rather than a passthrough: `meta` forwards, `room-reject` is consumed and aimed (section 6), the other four are dropped and counted, and the count is flushed once per heartbeat as `relay.misaddressed-frame` rather than logged per frame, because the publisher chooses the rate.

**A host's own control traffic is untouched**, which is the half that keeps this from being a cage: any `t` this library does not define, and anything that is not JSON at all, forwards exactly as before. The roster channel is the seam the library gives a host for its own broadcasts, and an allowlist that only knows the library's own frames is what makes that safe rather than merely permitted.

## 9. Two publishes and two roster seeds

The join sequence looks redundant. Both halves are load-bearing and answer different problems.

**The immediate publish, unconditional, at socket open.** Until the join envelope reaches the ticker, a reconnecting player can be **frozen server-side**: their inputs are accepted and stored while their entity does not move, and their client happily predicts forward the whole time. Gating this on the subscribe acknowledgement puts a fresh subscriber's TCP, TLS, AUTH and SUBSCRIBE in front of it, measured at over a second of freeze in an otherwise healthy reconnect.

**The publish gated on the subscribe ack.** The ticker announces a roster change **exactly once** (it only re-dirties its map when a join actually *changes* it, so later heartbeats announce nothing), and pub/sub drops a message with no subscriber. A socket whose subscription lands after its own announcement therefore misses itself **permanently**: the player is invisible in their own roster for the whole session.

**The roster is seeded twice** for the same reason, and the second seed is what makes it airtight, by ordering rather than by hoping:

> Redis is single-threaded, and the ticker does `HSET` on the meta hash **before** it `PUBLISH`es, on one connection. So a hash read issued strictly *after* our `SUBSCRIBE` was acknowledged cannot miss an announcement published before it. Either we receive the publish, or the `HSET` preceded it and our later read sees it. There is no third case.

Relatedly: the ticker's dirty flag **starts true**, and it is **re-set when a roster publish is rejected by the bus**. A predecessor that died between its hash write and its metaout publish strands that announcement forever otherwise, because every later heartbeat matches the restored map and never re-dirties it.

**Every join and leave carries `c`, the relay's own connection id**, and a leave from a connection the player has already replaced is ignored. Sockets are swapped routinely (a reconnect, and the planned swap at a relay's lifetime cap) and the order the two relays reach the bus in is not guaranteed: the replacement's join frequently lands before the old relay's close handler has fired, and honouring that close removes a player sitting in the room on a live socket. A leave carrying no `c`, and one for a pid with no recorded `c`, are honoured exactly as before, because there is nothing to contradict them with.

**The `room-reject` the ticker publishes carries the refused join's own `c` back, and ABSENT is the only thing that means "no connection named".** Section 6 is about why a pid is not a fine enough aim; this is the shape of the test. The relay drops a reject whose pid is not its own, and then drops one whose `c` is present and does not match. A reject with **no** `c` at all is an older ticker and is still honoured on pid alone, because there is nothing more precise to match with. What that cannot be is a `typeof c === 'string'` check: a wrong-typed `c` (a number, an object, anything a fuzzer or a version skew produces) read as absent under that test, fell back to matching on the pid, and closed **both** of a swapping player's sockets, which is exactly the regression the field exists to prevent. A `c` that is present and does not match this connection is not ours, whatever shape it arrived in.

**A socket still CONNECTING publishes neither join**, which is why the pair above stays correct rather than becoming a hole: `startSession` runs after the subscribe ack in that ordering, so its own single seed already has the property the two publishes exist to give.

### A player is present because a relay keeps saying so

The relay heartbeats its join every second and the only removal was one fire-and-forget `leave`, which pub/sub drops when nothing is subscribed, which is precisely when a relay is most likely to be closing sockets: **in a handoff gap**. The phantom stayed in the roster, in the meta hash, and in every successor's restored checkpoint, holding a capacity slot no room could ever reclaim. A pid whose heartbeat has stopped for `presenceTimeoutMs` (5000, at least three heartbeats, so a single lost beat is never a departure) gets the leave the bus dropped, synthesised by the ticker on a cadence nothing on the wire can drive, and carrying no `c` so it is honoured like a relay's own.

The other half of the same fact is that **removal follows the SIMULATION's membership, not the socket's**. A `leave` means a socket closed, which is not the same event as a player leaving: a simulation with a disconnect grace deliberately keeps a departed player for several seconds so their reconnect resumes seamlessly. Deleting them from `present` on the socket's timetable meant the reconnect arrived as a **new** player, met `isFull`, and was answered with `room-reject` while the simulation still had them sitting in the room, so the grace period being kept for them was exactly what they could not use. `presentPids` owns both removals now, which is the authority the metadata already followed; a runtime that does not implement it keeps today's behaviour unchanged.

## 10. The relay answers the ping itself

`RoomConnection` measures its round trip with a `ping` frame the **relay** answers directly, without touching Redis, without decoding anything, and without waiting for the ticker. That is the whole point: anything the relay does on the way is measurement error added to the number, and the number now drives the stamping lead, which makes a fake one actively harmful rather than merely useless. The frame it replaces measured nothing at all. It paired the oldest outstanding `send()` with the next *snapshot*, so a host sending at the tick rate into a room publishing at the tick rate measured roughly U(0, tickMs), and a player on satellite and a player on fibre reported the same "RTT".

Three details, all of them load-bearing:

- **It sits after the token bucket, not before.** A ping is a frame like any other and pays a token, so an abuser cannot buy extra allowance by relabelling a flood.
- **It is recognised by a literal prefix**, `{"t":"ping"`, before `decodeInput` runs, and only for a frame small enough to be one. A full utf8 decode of every inbound frame to find out whether it was a ping is exactly the amplifier this file spends a token bucket avoiding. The cost of that shortcut is one rule for hosts: **your own input frames must not begin that way.**
- **Both echoed fields are checked, not trusted.** `n` and `c` come straight back to the client, and a hand-crafted `NaN` or `Infinity` would return through `JSON.stringify` as a literal `null` and poison the client's own arithmetic.
- **And the client MATCHES the pong to an outstanding ping rather than timing the newest one.** `n` is a sequence number, and the client keeps the send time of each ping it has not seen answered yet; a pong is a sample only if its `n` names one of them, and that entry is then consumed. Without the match, a duplicated, reordered or fabricated pong is timed against whichever send happened to be newest, which is a sample of nothing. The outstanding map is bounded by `RTT_WINDOW` for the same reason every other buffer here is bounded: a relay that simply stops answering must not grow anything.

It costs the room nothing: no Redis command, no publish, and a 2-second cadence per socket.

**The number the client reports out of those samples is a sliding-window minimum, not an average**, and the argument is the same one section 5 makes twice for the two clock estimators: every sample is the true path time plus whatever queueing happened on the way, and queueing is non-negative, so the smallest sample in the window is the least contaminated estimate available. `RTT_WINDOW` is 8. Two samples are refused outright rather than minimised over. One above `RTT_MAX_SAMPLE_MS` (5000) is not a round trip anybody is playing across, and a hand-crafted echo measured 60000. And one taken while the **render loop was frozen** measures the freeze rather than the link: under an EMA, a 3 second freeze on a 20ms link reported `rttMs` **935** and, because that number feeds the stamping lead, snapped the tick counter **18 ticks forward** on the frame the tab came back. Confirmed in a real browser: across a 5 second rAF freeze the pings kept flowing (the socket is below page JavaScript) and every sample taken across the gap was refused by design.

### The reconnect ladder, sized by the same measurement

That number is also what says how long a reconnect should wait, and the first delay was wrong for years because nobody had measured the thing it was covering. `RECONNECT_BASE_MS` is **100**, times `RECONNECT_FACTOR` **2** per attempt, capped at `maxBackoffMs` (5000), and each delay is then multiplied by a jitter factor drawn uniformly from `[RECONNECT_JITTER_MIN 0.5, RECONNECT_JITTER_MAX 1.5)`. All four are exported from the client barrel, because a constant a test reaches through a deep import is a constant a consumer cannot reach at all.

**Base 100 rather than 250, because the ladder was most of the outage it was pacing.** Measured in headless Chromium, a healthy host opens the replacement socket in **6 to 18ms**, while the interpolator's cover is its delay (80ms at the floor) plus `EXTRAP_CAP_MS` (150), so 230ms. A flat 250ms first retry sat *outside* that cover by itself, and every reconnect on a fast link therefore ended in about four motionless frames caused by nothing but the backoff. 100 fits inside the cover with room to spare and still reaches the same cap within one extra step.

**Jitter, because this architecture builds a thundering herd by construction.** One relay function holds many sockets and dies all at once, so every client on that instance starts its ladder in the same millisecond and retries in lockstep for as long as they keep failing: measured on two clients dropped together, both scheduled at exactly `[250, 250]`. The cap applies to the exponential term and the jitter rides on top, so a fully backed-off fleet retries across a 3:1 spread rather than all landing on the cap together. The pre-open re-mint threshold stays keyed on **attempts** rather than elapsed time, precisely because the delays are now random and anything keyed on the clock would fire at a different point on every client.

## 11. Telling the player the world is gone

A room whose ticker hangs is worse than one that crashes, because a crash self-heals (the relay's poll spawns a successor) and a hang does not: the hung ticker still holds its lease, so nobody may replace it. Meanwhile every client's socket stays `OPEN` and the world simply stops. Measured in production: 17+ seconds of a frozen world with the socket healthy and nothing on screen.

`stallDecision` closes it with one predicate covering all three ways it happens:

- an established stream that stops (a hung or dead ticker),
- a joiner landing in a room that is **already** hung, which is every new joiner once a room hangs,
- a reconnect loop that never resumes.

Silence is measured from **the last evidence the world is alive**: the newest snapshot, or, before any has ever arrived, the moment this client first tried to connect. That single origin is what makes one predicate cover all three.

**Two thresholds, chosen by whether the current connection has delivered anything.** 4000ms for a live stream that stopped, which sits above the handoff budget so a routine healthy handoff never raises the banner. 8000ms otherwise, because a cold join legitimately takes seconds (a relay cold start, a ticker spawn, simulation init).

**Hysteresis matters.** Once stalled, the tight limit applies regardless, so only a real snapshot clears the state. Without it, a hung room whose socket then dropped would flick the message off as the window widened and back on moments later.

**Socket readiness is deliberately not in the predicate.** A closed socket in backoff is exactly as silent to the player as an open one behind a hung ticker, and hiding the message the moment the socket dropped removes it precisely when things got worse. That makes the terminal latches load-bearing rather than belt-and-braces: they are now the only thing keeping this off a state that already owns the screen.

The terminals are `'closed-by-server'`, `'capacity'`, `'conn-limit'`, `'version-skew'`, `'connect-error'`, `'mint-failed'` and `'stopped'`. Three things about that list are worth knowing:

- **`'conn-limit'` was called `'rate-limited'`**, which named the wrong mechanism entirely: `CLOSE_CODES.connLimit` and the `conn-limit` frame both mean the per-subject *socket* cap (already playing in another tab), not a message rate limit, and both examples' banner text already said so.
- **`'connect-error'` and `'mint-failed'` exist because two paths could previously fail forever in silence.** The ladder is driven by a `void`ed promise, so a throw in the post-mint half of an attempt was an unhandled rejection and the ladder simply stopped: no timer, no terminal, status stuck on `'connecting'`. And nothing bounded `mint()` or the handshake at all, measured at ten minutes of fake time with the connecting banner up and no attempt in flight. Both now have deadlines that feed the *ordinary* ladder, and both give up loudly after three.
- **`CLOSE_CODES.relayUnavailable` (4004) is deliberately not on the list.** A relay that could not subscribe, or that reached its own lifetime cap, is a fact about one relay and not about this player's session.

**Every refusal latches AND closes.** The terminal used to latch while the socket stayed live, so a client that had been told the room was full went on receiving and rendering that room's snapshots, with the banner up, for as long as the relay felt like keeping it. `room-full`, `conn-limit` and an own-pid `room-reject` all do both now, and a host can still restart deliberately with `start({ remint: true })`, which is what makes the balancer's bounded re-assign expressible against this class at all.

**Which makes the ORDER inside a terminal load-bearing, and it was wrong.** The restart above runs from inside `onTerminal`, and `start()` reaches `new Impl(url)` synchronously, so announcing the terminal *before* tearing the old socket down closed the socket the restart had just created. Measured: two sockets, the restart's `readyState` 3, `reconnects` 0, dead forever, and the failure was invisible from the outside because a bare 4002 close code, which announces nothing and therefore restarts nothing, survived the same path perfectly. `enterTerminal` latches, tears down, drops the held poses, settles the status, and calls `onTerminal` **last**. A callback a host is expected to act *from* is a handover rather than a notification, and a handover has to leave the object in the state the receiver will act on. The same restart takes the non-awaited path, so an unusable re-mint counts toward `badMints` and latches `'mint-failed'` rather than rejecting a promise nobody is holding; only a first, awaited `start()` throws, where the caller wants the stack trace.

**And every call out to host code goes through one wrapper**, for the reason the `void`ed-promise bullet above already gives from the other end: `onStatus`, `onStallChange`, `onTerminal`, `onTickReanchor`, `onText` and `onSnapshot` are all called inside a catch, and the close path schedules the reconnect *before* it announces the status. Either one alone stops a throwing host callback from ending the ladder, which is why neither has a mutation of its own; both are kept because the ordering is free and the wrapper is one type test.

**Two more refusals on the inbound side, both of which are "finite is not plausible" again.** Once the server clock is seeded, a snapshot whose `serverTime` sits more than `SNAPSHOT_TIME_PLAUSIBLE_MS` (60s) from the client's own estimate of server now, or whose `tick` is more than `SNAPSHOT_TICK_JUMP_MAX` (1e6) from the last accepted one, is refused and counted on `rejectedSnapshots` exactly as a non-finite one is. And it has the same escape hatch the interpolator's floor refusal has, for the same reason: the reference both bounds are judged against is one this class computed for itself, so a reference that is wrong refuses every frame forever, and a room that genuinely restarted trips the test on every frame. A run past `SNAPSHOT_IMPLAUSIBLE_REFUSALS` (3) is therefore adopted as a new timeline. One difference from the interpolator, and it is the whole reason a *consecutive* count is safe here: a WebSocket delivers in order, so nothing reorders this stream. The interpolator's equivalent had to be windowed precisely because `push()` documents out-of-order arrivals as ordinary.

**The escape RE-ARMS at the adoption point, and leaving it latched was a hole big enough to drive the whole trust boundary through.** The run counter was cleared only by a *plausible* frame, so once a run had opened the gate it stayed open. Measured on 100 frames each stamped a random ±2e8ms away: 3 were refused and **97 were adopted**, which put `estimateServerTick()` at 5.6e6, `tick.value` at **-542716** (a negative stamp is an input the server can never place) and handed the host an `onTickReanchor` delta of 3,432,713. Re-armed, a genuinely sustained step costs three refusals and one adoption per run of four until the clock re-seeds, about a second, while noise never gets through for free. Same shape as `refuseSteppedFrame`'s own re-arm one module along, and the same lesson: an escape hatch is a rate, not a switch.

### The rate itself was the last silent thing

`tickHz` is required rather than defaulted, because a default was a silent 2x error. Requiring it only moved the failure: a host that writes 10 for a room running at 20 still gets no error, no stall and clean stats, while the tick counter's step, `estimateServerTick`'s slope and the underrun threshold all run at half basis. The only tells were `onTickReanchor` firing a same-signed delta every couple of seconds (the counter running at the wrong slope, dragged back on a timer it can never catch) and the server's playout depth pinned at 0, neither of which reads as "your `tickHz` is wrong" to anybody.

The server states its own rate on every pair of snapshots, since a tick delta and a `serverTime` delta *are* a rate, so the connection measures it and says so. `stats().serverTickHz` is the **median** of a 21-sample window rather than a mean, so one handoff-sized gap in the stream cannot move it, and only forward, in-order pairs contribute at all (a duplicate, a reorder or an adopted timeline restart is not a rate). `onTickRateMismatch(measuredHz)` fires **once per epoch**, after the measurement has sat more than `TICK_RATE_TOLERANCE` (a fifth, far wider than any jitter and far tighter than the 2x a wrong constant produces) from the configured rate for `TICK_RATE_MISMATCH_RUN` (40) consecutive pairs.

**It changes nothing else, deliberately.** The counter, the lead and the clock all keep running on the *configured* rate, because a class that quietly adopted the measured one would be back to guessing a number the host is supposed to know, and the fix for a wrong constant is telling the host rather than working around them.

**A relay swap is rate limited and its deadline has a floor.** `relay-expiring` is a frame a relay chooses to send, so a broken or hostile one can send it as fast as it likes: **201 sockets in 10 seconds**, measured. One swap per `RELAY_EXPIRY_LEAD_MS`, and the deadline is floored at `SWAP_MIN_DEADLINE_MS` (1000) so an announced `inMs` of 0 does not hand the replacement no time at all. That floor sits under the `min(connectTimeoutMs, announced)` rule above rather than replacing it.

**The first anchor of an epoch is provisional with respect to the round trip it assumed.** It is taken before the first pong exists, so it is computed with `rttMs` reading 0 and is short by the whole round trip; and because anchoring sets the rate limiter's own timestamp, the tolerance path then refuses to correct it for `REANCHOR_MIN_INTERVAL_MS`. Measured at 250ms RTT: the counter sat 5.2 to 5.8 ticks below where it wanted to be for **2.2 seconds**, the server counted **40 late inputs** in the first three seconds, and the correction that finally came overshot (+6, +3, then -3 at 8.65s). It reproduced in the first epoch of every run, which is every reconnect of every session. So a pong that moves `rttMs` a whole tick away from the value the anchor assumed drops the rate limit, and the next snapshot corrects it through the ordinary tolerance path with an ordinary `onTickReanchor`.

**Neither of the two "not yet" timestamps in that machinery may be 0.** `now()` is a `performance.now()` reading, so 0 is a real timestamp roughly two seconds before the earliest moment a page can have connected at all: the swap rate limit never engaged on a page that opened its socket immediately, and the tolerance re-anchor was disabled for the first two seconds of every page's life. Both are `Number.NEGATIVE_INFINITY`. This is the third time this repo has put a sentinel inside its own value's range, `PlayoutBuffer.aheadBase` being the one written up at length in `AGENTS.md`, which is enough times to make it a thing to look for rather than a thing to notice.

**A 4001 is two different events sharing one code**, and reading it as one made an expired token look like a kick. The node adapter verifies the token *after* the upgrade, so a session older than the host's `maxAgeS` ends the game on the next network blip. A 4001 arriving **before this epoch delivered anything** is therefore read as a stale token and three of them force a fresh mint; once the epoch has delivered, the server had already accepted this token, so a 4001 can only be a deliberate kick. It needs its own counter rather than riding `preOpenFailures`, because those sockets *do* open and `onopen` clears that counter, so counting there could never reach a threshold.

`stats()` reports `{ rttMs, jitterMs, snapshotsReceived, rejectedSnapshots, underrunRate, reconnects, relaySwaps, swapsAttempted, swapsFailed, serverTickHz, hostErrors }`, and the split in it is the same one the interpolator makes: **gauges are epoch-scoped, counters are lifetime.** A gauge carried across an epoch describes a connection that no longer exists, measured as a fresh socket's first snapshot reporting 435ms of jitter and an underrun rate of 0.49, both of them the shape of the outage rather than of the new socket, at the worst possible moment to be reporting a sick connection.

**The three swap counters are an identity, and that is what makes them readable.** `swapsAttempted - relaySwaps - swapsFailed` is 0, or 1 while a replacement is still in flight, so the pair to watch is `swapsFailed` against `swapsAttempted`: a `relaySwaps` of 0 says nothing on its own (a room whose relays never reach their cap has nothing to swap), while the two of them climbing together says every lifetime cap in the deployment is still costing the visible reconnect the warm swap exists to remove. Section 1 has the usual cause, which is a session shorter than the relay lifetime chain.

**`hostErrors` is the client's half of `guardHost`.** Every call out to host code goes through `emit()`, and `interpolate.entities` is guarded separately because it has a return value (a throw has to skip the push specifically and let everything after it happen). Surviving a throwing callback is the right behaviour; being *silent* about it is not, and this class has no logger to be loud with, so the count is the signal. A healthy client reads 0 for its whole lifetime.

The message is **non-blocking**. The state usually self-heals, so the player keeps control of a live world while it does.

## 12. Wire format

Snapshots are published at 20Hz and delivered once per player. Bytes here are the bill.

- **Binary, not JSON.** Typically 5 to 20x smaller for the same content.
- **Quantise.** Positions to centimetre-precision `i16` (a ±327m range), angles to `u16` turns. Pick the smallest field that covers your world plus headroom.
- **Clamp, never wrap.** An out-of-range value that *wraps* teleports an entity to the opposite side of the world, which reads as a catastrophic desync. The same value *clamped* pins it at the boundary, which reads as an entity pressed against a wall. One is a bug report; the other is a shrug.
- **...and one layer down, an integer field REFUSES rather than either.** Clamping is right for a coordinate because the clamped value still reads correctly; a state enum, a target tick or a byte length has no nearby value. `ByteWriter`'s integer setters used to hand the number straight to `DataView`, which wraps silently: `state 300` arrived as 44, `targetTick -1` as 4294967295, and a 70000-byte extra decoded as 4464 bytes. They now throw `CodecError` on a non-integer or out-of-range value, so the writer is a trust boundary the way the reader always has been.
- **NaN is refused too, and for a reason clamping cannot cover.** `NaN < min` and `NaN > max` are both false, so a clamp falls straight through and `DataView.setInt16(NaN)` stores 0: a NaN position teleports its entity to the world origin with no error anywhere. There is no direction to clamp NaN toward, unlike `±Infinity`, which still clamps to the ends of the range.
- **Version the wire, and compare the version first.**

### The version-bump rule that is easy to get wrong

Bump the protocol version when the wire changes **meaning**, not only when it changes **shape**.

Adding a new value to an existing enum moves no byte and widens no field. A mismatched client does not crash; it reads a value it has no case for and silently does the wrong thing. Re-ordering an enum is worse: not one byte moves and not one value is new, but the same number now means something else.

**The test is whether a mismatched pair would misread each other, not whether the offsets moved.** Silently wrong is exactly what the version field exists to catch.

### Which decoders throw and which return empty, because it is not one rule

This section used to say "every decode checks the version before reading a single field", and that was the intent rather than the behaviour: `decodeDefaultSnapshot` read fields first. It checks the version first now. But the two decoders answer a mismatch **differently on purpose**, and the difference follows from what the frame is:

- **A snapshot decoder THROWS `ProtocolVersionError`.** A snapshot is the whole world; a client that cannot read one has nothing to render and needs to *recover*, which for a browser client means reloading onto the new bundle. `ProtocolVersionError` is a `CodecError` subtype carrying `expected`/`found`, with `name === 'ProtocolVersionError'`, and `RoomConnection` duck-types on that name so a host's own codec can participate without importing anything from this library.
- **An input-window decoder returns `[]`.** An input frame is one of many arriving at the tick rate, the next one supersedes it, and a throw there is the relay's `onBadInput` abuse signal rather than a decode result.

**Version-skew recovery could not fire for a throwing decoder at all**, which is the defect this pairing came out of. The client's skew check needed a decoder that *decodes* a frame it does not understand and reports the version in the result; this repo's own pong codec throws instead, and the connection's catch swallowed it. A deploy that bumped the wire therefore left every old client silently dropping every frame, with nothing reloading and nothing latching.

The decoder is a **trust boundary** in every other respect too. Every section stops at the bytes actually present; a count field is clamped to both its declared maximum and the bytes available, so a crafted `count: 255` cannot amplify into hundreds of allocations; and a truncated packet decodes to empty rather than throwing, so one malformed frame costs nothing.

**The trust boundary extends past the codec, into whatever the host wrote.** `RoomConnection.processSnapshot` refuses a non-finite `tick` or `serverTime` before any accumulator sees it, counting it on `rejectedSnapshots`, for exactly the reason `SnapshotInterpolator.push()` does the same: the field is typed `number` and the value crossed a decode boundary the host owns. One frame with an undefined `serverTime` used to put NaN in the clock EMA, which can never leave NaN, after which `estimateServerTick()` was NaN for the rest of the connection, `shouldReanchor` could never fire again (every comparison against NaN is false), and after the next reconnect every stamped input carried NaN.

## 13. What to measure

Two numbers precede almost every real incident:

- **`starves`**: playout buffer misses. Rising means clients and server disagree about the tick timeline.
- **`renewFails`**: lease renewal failures. Rising means a split-brain guard is about to fire, or should be.

Two more worth watching:

- **`tickHz`**, measured against **real wall time between flushes**, never assumed. A stalled loop is exactly what the number exists to expose, and computing it from the loop's own assumptions hides the failure.
- **`bytesDelivered`** = `bytesPublished × playerCount`, the fan-out that the bandwidth bill actually tracks.

Three more that the audit added, each because a real failure had no number at all:

- **`publishSkipped`**: snapshots the loop dropped rather than queue behind a slow bus. Distinct from `publishFails`, which is a publish that was issued and rejected.
- **`hostErrors`**: throws and rejected promises out of the host's own hooks, which the room now survives. Rising means there is a bug in the simulation that nothing else will tell you about, since the loop deliberately keeps running without those calls.
- **`lateInputs`**: playout re-stamps that landed, as a per-window delta.

And two more that the completeness round added, both for the same reason as the three above:

- **`refusedInputs`**: stamps sitting further AHEAD of the consumed floor than `playoutMaxAhead` allows, discarded rather than buffered. Rising means a sender's lead is larger than this room is configured to hold, which is a configuration fault rather than a network one, and it is the only thing that separates that cause from every other reason `starves` climbs. Its counterpart is `lateInputs`, which is where a sender running BEHIND shows up; nothing behind the floor is ever refused.
- **`rejectsSuppressed`**: `room-reject` frames NOT published because one had already gone to that pid within the last second. The reject rides the roster channel, which fans out to every socket in the room, so a producer that can choose the join rate would otherwise be choosing the room's broadcast rate too; the rate limit is `REJECT_INTERVAL_MS` (1000) per pid and this is what it cost.

### The log lines worth alerting on

`ticker.crash-loop`, `ticker.input-dead`, `ticker.checkpoint-refused-not-owner` and `ticker.presence-timeout` each name a failure that used to be entirely silent. `ticker.host-errors` and `ticker.unknown-envelope` are the once-per-flush summaries of the two counters above (never per message: both sit on paths a client's traffic reaches). `ticker.setup-threw`, `ticker.subscribe-failed`, `ticker.checkpoint-read-failed` and `ticker.subscriber-error` cover the setup half, which previously either threw out of `runTicker` with the lease still held or carried on regardless. `ticker.lease-lost` now carries `meta.finder` (`guard`, `renew`, `checkpoint` or `setup`), which is what tells a Redis restart apart from a slow loop, and `ticker.host-errors` carries the `hook` that threw, which is what makes the once-per-flush summary actionable: `onEvents` fires every tick, so its own per-call line was **41 lines for 41 ticks** and is now counted and named in that summary like every other hook. The only per-call host-error lines left are `onStats` (once per flush) and `metaPayload` (once per roster change), both cadences nothing on the wire can drive. On the relay: `relay.backlog-drop`, `relay.subscriber-dead` (a subscriber that ended, or one whose own probe went unanswered), `relay.lifetime-reached`, `relay.socket-cap-unevaluated`, `relay.open-timeout` (a socket that never left CONNECTING), `relay.no-ping` (once at attach, a transport with no `ping()`, so the liveness deadline is running on plain inbound recency: see section 8), and two per-heartbeat summaries carrying a `count` rather than a line per event, `relay.send-failed` and `relay.misaddressed-frame` (a per-socket library frame published on the broadcast roster channel). `ticker.room-normalised` and `relay.room-normalised` are the pair to alert on across routes rather than within one: a room id refused and replaced is usually the three `maxRooms` values disagreeing (section 6), and it is the only symptom that failure has. `ticker.timeline-write-failed` is the one to watch on the crash path specifically: the marker that keeps a successor's stamps strictly above everything the thrown predecessor published did not go down.

**`relay.lifetime-reached` is a line whose ABSENCE is the healthy reading**, which is worth knowing before hunting for it. It is logged only when the relay reaches its cap with the socket still attached, i.e. when the client did *not* swap in time, so a working warm swap produces no server-side line at all. `conn.stats().relaySwaps` climbing while `reconnects` stays at 0 is the observable, and it is the one a browser run confirmed: four swaps completing in 3 to 52ms, zero reconnects, the status never leaving `open`, and not one `relay.lifetime-reached` on the host. At the outermost seam: `relay.upgrade-threw` and `node-relay.connection`, where a throw out of the platform's own callback is logged, closed 1011 and resolved rather than escaping into a host's request handler. Every statement inside those two catch blocks is itself guarded, because a throwing host logger would re-raise the rejection being swallowed.

### Attributing a gap, which takes both sides of the socket

A client measuring its own snapshot arrivals can see a gap and can say nothing at all about where it came from: a slow ticker, a slow bus, a relay function paused between snapshots and a slow socket all read identically from a browser. That is exactly how a real deployment's 250 to 433ms arrival band sat unattributed while an in-function probe measured the Redis path at a p99 of 2.35ms.

So the relay measures the same quantity on its own side. Per socket, per heartbeat window, it records the inter-arrival gap on its **own subscriber** (`busGapMax`, and `busGapOver150`, which carries its threshold in its name because that is the field name on the line as well) and the time from a bus arrival to the send returning (`sendLagMax`), and logs **one** `relay.gaps` line at info only when `busGapMax` is over 150 or `sendLagMax` over 50. `RelayHandle.gapsSample()` exposes the window in progress for a host that would rather read it on its own cadence.

**The reading rule is the whole point, and half of it is the absence of a line.** A `relay.gaps` line whose `busGapMax` matches a gap a client reported puts the cause **upstream of the socket**: the ticker, the bus, or this relay's own function being paused. A client gap with **no** relay line beside it is the **socket path**, because the bytes reached the relay on time. A healthy socket logs nothing, deliberately, like every other summary here.

**The first run of that rule read the second way, and an absent line is only evidence once the capture is proven.** Ten minutes, three clients: eight client-side arrival gaps over 250ms, and no `relay.gaps` line at all in the platform's log for that window, with other relay lines from the same deployment present in it, which is what makes the absence a measurement rather than a logging failure. Against an in-function probe that had already put the Redis path at a p99 of about 2ms, that leaves the socket path or the client's own loop.

**The client half closed it, and the instrument is worth copying.** The limit of everything above is that a client's arrival times are inferred from rendered frames, so a stalled renderer and a delayed packet are the same reading. The fix is a ring of timestamps taken in a socket `message` listener registered **before** the library assigns its own `onmessage`, with the clock read as the handler's first statement, so what is recorded is the arrival rather than the arrival plus a decode. Measured that way over ten minutes and three clients: about 12,000 socket arrivals each, a median gap of 49.8ms on a 50ms grid, a p99 of 69 to 75ms, and **every** frame-inferred gap over 250ms confirmed at the socket within a few milliseconds, none of them explained by the render loop, with no `relay.gaps` line in the window. The band is therefore between the relay's `send` returning and the browser's `message` event, which is a platform property; the lever left to a host is the interpolation delay floor. **One ambiguity is irreducible from inside the page** and should be stated rather than papered over: a whole-process stall stops the message handler too, so a confirmation at the socket means "not only the render loop" rather than "the network". Closing that needs a second ring on a plain interval, measuring the page's own scheduling.

### A counter counts what happened, and an empty window is not a healthy one

Two rules that sound like tidiness and are the difference between a metric and a lie.

**Counters move inside the publish promise, never beside it.** `publishes`, `bytesPublished` and `bytesDelivered` record deliveries, not attempts. A room whose every publish is rejected otherwise reports 20 publishes a second with bytes climbing at the healthy rate.

**An empty latency window is `null`, never zeros.** For a distribution of durations, zero is the *best* possible value, so flattening "no samples at all" to `{ p50: 0, p95: 0, max: 0 }` makes the sickest room in the fleet report the healthiest numbers in it. `RoomStats.cadence`, `publishAwait` and `serverInternal` are `Percentiles | null` for that reason. The general shape: when a gauge's empty value is also its healthiest value, the empty case has to be a different **type**, not a different number.

### The alerting blind spot

A ticker that dies **hard** does not report a bad value. Its room **disappears** from the metrics entirely, because every per-room gauge is derived from the stats key that the ticker itself writes. Per-room alerts then see NoData, which is usually mapped to OK, and nothing pages.

The fix is a signal that does not depend on the ticker being alive. Have the **relay** register socket presence on a slow timer (a global sorted set, re-scored periodically so a hard function kill ages out rather than leaking), then join it against the stats keys server-side. **Sockets registered to a room with no stats key** is the alert: it means players are connected to a room that nothing is simulating.

Note that this state is hard to *test* by killing tickers, because ticker death self-heals every time (the successor spawn and the relay poll race, and one of them always wins). To reproduce it, **squat the lease**: hold it without ticking. That is a hung ticker rather than a dead one, and it is the failure this gauge actually exists to catch.

**Half of that blind spot is now covered in-process, and the half that is covered is the one nothing outside could see.** A ticker whose input subscription is black-holed is not hung and not dead: it holds the lease, ticks, publishes, renews, and writes a perfectly healthy stats key, while every join and every input is silently dropped. Measured over a 4 second black hole: **zero log lines, healthy stats, one input applied**. No external join can find that, because every signal it would join on reads fine. The only way to learn that a channel still delivers is to send something down it and watch for it coming back, so the ticker publishes its own `probe` on its input channel every `PROBE_INTERVAL_MS` (1s) and reads the answer through its own subscriber; a subscription silent for `PROBE_DEAD_MS` (3s) is `'input-dead'`, which releases and hands off like a duration cap, because the subscription is opened once per run and only a fresh process repairs it. Both are their own constants rather than multiples of `statsMs`: a knob documented as a metrics cadence must not be able to decide when a room dies, and as a multiple it did, so `statsMs: 40` gave a healthy room a 120ms liveness deadline. The probe carries the publishing ticker's owner id and answers only to itself: two tickers overlap on one room for a moment around a handoff, and without that filter a successor's probes would answer for a predecessor whose subscription had already died, so the one signal built to catch this would be the thing hiding it.

**A Redis restart without persistence is the case that stays open**, and it is documented rather than fixed. Every lease vanishes at once, so several tickers can each acquire and publish until each predecessor's next renew comes back false: roughly **one second of two authorities interleaving**, bounded and self-correcting. Closing it entirely needs a fencing token on every write, which is a different architecture. What changed is that it is no longer silent: `ticker.lease-lost` with `meta.finder` says which detector fired, where before a whole fleet exiting `'lease-lost'` produced not one line.

## 14. What this deliberately is not

**Not lockstep.** Peers never wait on each other. State-synchronised with client prediction, which degrades gracefully under loss where lockstep stalls.

**Not a CRDT.** There is one authority and it is the server. For text or documents with no natural authority, use a CRDT.

**Not zero-latency.** Other entities render 80 to 500ms behind, and near the 80ms floor whenever the network is calm. That delay is what buys smoothness. It is the correct trade for everything except a competitive shooter, which wants lag compensation and rewind, a different architecture.

**Not delta-compressed.** Snapshots are full state. That is why loss is free (the next snapshot supersedes the last), and it is why the wire format matters. Deltas would cut bandwidth and reintroduce a dependency on every prior packet, which is a much worse failure mode. Revisit only when `bytesDelivered` says to.
